## ⚠️ When to see a doctor

**Palpitations with chest pain, shortness of breath, or feeling faint or fainting are an emergency.** The document below has a form called "Palpitations with trouble sleeping". The NHS advises calling emergency services or going to A&E right away if you currently have heart palpitations that do not go away, or palpitations with any of these: **chest pain, shortness of breath, or feeling faint or fainting** — and not to drive yourself there. If you had these symptoms but they have now stopped, the NHS advises getting an urgent appointment. Palpitations — a heartbeat that feels racing, irregular, pounding or fluttering — are usually harmless, but see a doctor if they keep coming back or happen more often, last longer than a few minutes, or if you have a heart condition or a family history of heart problems.

The NHS advises seeing a doctor if changing your sleeping habits has not helped, you have had trouble sleeping for months, or insomnia is affecting your daily life in a way that makes it hard to cope.

The NHS advises seeing a doctor if you have any of the main symptoms of sleep apnoea, such as **breathing that stops and starts** while you sleep, gasping, snorting or choking noises while you sleep, or always feeling very tired during the day — because it can be serious if it is not diagnosed and treated. Loud snoring is also one of the symptoms the NHS lists. Do not drive when you feel sleepy (NHS). Check with your doctor before taking anything for your sleep (NHS).

## What insomnia is

According to the NHS (UK National Health Service), insomnia means regularly having problems sleeping: finding it hard to go to sleep, waking several times during the night, lying awake, waking early and not getting back to sleep, still feeling tired after waking, and feeling tired, irritable and unable to concentrate during the day. Insomnia lasting less than 3 months is called short-term insomnia; 3 months or longer is long-term insomnia.

The U.S. National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute (NHLBI) describes insomnia as a common sleep disorder that happens **even when you have the time and the right environment to sleep well**. Chronic insomnia occurs 3 or more nights a week, lasts more than 3 months, and cannot be fully explained by another health problem. According to the NHLBI, waking often during the night is the most common symptom and mostly affects older adults, while lying awake a long time before falling asleep is more common in younger adults. Insomnia can affect memory and concentration and can make people feel anxious, depressed or easily annoyed.

Sleep needs differ from person to person. On average, according to the NHS, adults need 7 to 9 hours, children 9 to 13 hours, and toddlers and babies 12 to 17 hours; if you are constantly tired during the day, you are probably not getting enough sleep.

## Common causes and the medical approach

The NHS lists the most common causes: stress, anxiety or depression; noise; a room that is too hot or cold; an uncomfortable bed; alcohol, caffeine or nicotine; illegal drugs; jet lag; and shift work. Some conditions and medicines can also cause insomnia — among them an overactive thyroid, menopause, restless legs syndrome, Alzheimer's disease and Parkinson's disease. Long-term pain, sleep apnoea and nightmares can also keep you from a good night's sleep. The NHLBI adds lifestyle habits that can raise the risk of sleep problems: changing your normal routine often, including your sleep schedule; interruptions in your sleep, such as waking up often to care for a baby; taking long naps during the day; getting too little physical activity during the day; and watching TV or using electronic devices close to bedtime. Insomnia can develop at any age, but the chances of having it increase as you get older (NHLBI).

According to the NHS, insomnia usually gets better by changing sleeping habits. A doctor will try to find out what is causing it so you get the right treatment, and may offer **cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia (CBT-I)**. The NHS says sleeping pills are now rarely prescribed. Sleeping pills can have serious side effects and people can become dependent on them. They are only prescribed for a few days, or weeks at most, if: the insomnia is very bad; other treatments have not worked. The NHLBI notes that chronic insomnia raises the risk of high blood pressure, coronary heart disease, diabetes and cancer.

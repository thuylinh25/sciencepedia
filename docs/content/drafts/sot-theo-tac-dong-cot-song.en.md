## ⚠️ When a fever is an emergency

**A child having a febrile seizure for the first time: call emergency services.** The NHS advises calling emergency services or going to the emergency department if a child has a seizure for the first time, a seizure lasts more than 5 minutes, the child has difficulty breathing, stiffness and twitching on only one side of the body, is more sleepy than usual for more than 1 hour after the seizure, or has more than one seizure within 24 hours. If the child is still having a seizure or is unconscious, call emergency services rather than driving. While you wait: note when the seizure starts and ends, protect the child's head with your hands or something soft, and remove dangerous objects; do **not** move the child or try to hold them in place, do **not** put anything in their mouth, and do not undress them or sponge them with cold water to lower their temperature (NHS). The document below includes a passage describing placing a hand on the back of a child who is having a seizure — that is an observation of the method, not a way to manage the seizure, and it does not replace calling emergency services.

On its meningitis page, the NHS advises calling emergency services or going to the emergency department if an adult or child aged 5 or over: has a very high or very low temperature, feels hot or cold to the touch, or is shivery; has a very painful headache; is confused, has slurred speech, or is not making sense; has a stiff neck and cannot look at bright lights; has a rash that does not fade when you press it; has a seizure for the first time; or has symptoms you are worried might be meningitis. On its sepsis page, the NHS adds: breathing very fast; blue, pale or blotchy skin.

The NHS advises calling emergency services when a child with a fever also has: **a stiff neck**; a rash that does not fade when a glass is pressed against it; discomfort with light; unusually cold hands and feet; blue, grey, paler than usual or blotchy skin, or blue, grey or paler than usual lips or tongue; **is drowsy and hard to wake**; extreme agitation (not stopping crying) or confusion; difficulty breathing (grunting noises, the stomach sucking under the ribcage), breathlessness or very fast breathing; or is not responding like they normally do, or is not interested in feeding or normal activities. A baby under 3 months with a temperature of 38°C or more, or 3–6 months with 39°C or more, needs urgent medical advice.

**Malaria is an emergency** and needs quick treatment with antimalarial medicines; you can die from it if it is not diagnosed and treated quickly (NHS). Anyone with a fever after being in a malaria area during the last 12 months should get medical help urgently. **Typhoid** is treated with antibiotics; a sudden stomach pain that keeps getting worse, black or dark red stools, or vomiting blood or material that looks like ground coffee are reasons to call emergency services (NHS).

## What is a fever?

According to the NHS (UK National Health Service), a high temperature is usually considered to be 38°C or above; this is sometimes called a fever. A high temperature is not usually a sign of anything serious — it is often just caused by the body fighting an infection, such as a cold or flu — but it could sometimes be a sign of something more serious if the temperature is very high or will not come down (NHS). Fever is very common in young children and usually returns to normal within 1 to 4 days (NHS).

Adults with a high temperature should get lots of rest and drink plenty of fluids (NHS). The NHS advises asking for an urgent GP appointment or getting help from NHS 111 if you have been treating a high temperature at home but it is not getting better or is getting worse.

## Febrile seizures in children

A febrile seizure can sometimes happen when a child has a high temperature, usually between 6 months and 6 years old, and usually lasts 2 to 3 minutes (NHS). The child may become stiff, twitch or jerk, become unconscious, not respond, and be sleepy or irritable for up to 1 hour afterwards. After a first febrile seizure, the child needs to be checked in hospital (NHS).

On consequences, the NHS says a febrile seizure can seem frightening, but it is unlikely to harm the child or cause long-term effects. NINDS (U.S. National Institute of Neurological Disorders and Stroke) says simple febrile seizures — the most common type, lasting a few seconds up to 15 minutes and happening only once in 24 hours — do not cause long-term health problems; even longer seizures are generally harmless, but they do increase the risk of developing epilepsy. NINDS also notes that results from an NINDS-funded study have suggested that prolonged febrile seizures may injure the brain. Children who have had a febrile seizure have a slightly increased chance of epilepsy later in life, but this is rare (NHS). Meningitis can cause both fever and seizures that look like febrile seizures, but these are much more serious (NINDS) — which is why the child must be checked even after the seizure has stopped: NINDS advises taking the child to the doctor afterwards to check for the cause of the fever, especially urgently if the child shows symptoms of meningitis such as a stiff neck, extreme sleepiness, breathing problems or a lot of vomiting.

## Malaria and typhoid

**Malaria** is a serious infection spread by the bite of an infected mosquito, found in tropical regions including large areas of Africa and Asia (NHS). Symptoms include a high temperature or feeling hot, cold or shivery; sweating; headache and confusion; feeling very tired and sleepy (especially in children); feeling or being sick, tummy pain and diarrhoea; and jaundice. Symptoms usually appear 7 to 18 days after the bite (NHS). Malaria is treated with antimalarial medicines (NHS). According to the WHO, some types of malaria can cause severe illness and death; severe symptoms include extreme tiredness and fatigue, impaired consciousness, multiple convulsions, difficulty breathing, dark or bloody urine, jaundice and abnormal bleeding — people with severe symptoms should get emergency care right away.

**Typhoid fever** is an infection spread through unclean food or water (NHS). Symptoms include a high temperature that may slowly get worse over a few days, headache, cough, chills, tiredness, constipation and loss of appetite; tummy ache and diarrhoea usually come later. It is treated with antibiotics, and the full course must be finished even if you feel better (NHS). Untreated, typhoid can lead to internal bleeding or peritonitis (NHS); the WHO says severe cases may lead to serious complications or even death.

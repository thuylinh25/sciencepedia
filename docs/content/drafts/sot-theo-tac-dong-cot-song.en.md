## ⚠️ When a fever is an emergency

**A child having a febrile seizure for the first time: call emergency services.** The NHS advises calling emergency services or going to the emergency department if a child has a seizure for the first time, a seizure lasts more than 5 minutes, the child has difficulty breathing, stiffness and twitching on only one side of the body, is more sleepy than usual for more than 1 hour after the seizure, or has more than one seizure within 24 hours. If the child is still having a seizure or is unconscious, call emergency services rather than driving. While you wait: note when the seizure starts and ends, protect the child's head with your hands or something soft, and remove dangerous objects; do **not** move the child or try to hold them in place, do **not** put anything in their mouth, and do not undress them or sponge them with cold water to lower their temperature (NHS). The document below includes a passage describing placing a hand on the back of a child who is having a seizure — that is an observation of the method, not a way to manage the seizure, and it does not replace calling emergency services.

At any age, MedlinePlus advises calling emergency services for a fever together with: a child crying who cannot be calmed; being hard or impossible to wake; seeming confused; being unable to walk; difficulty breathing even after the nose is cleared; blue lips, tongue or nails; a very bad headache; a stiff neck; a child refusing to move an arm or leg; or a seizure.

The NHS advises calling emergency services when a child with a fever also has: a rash that does not fade when a glass is pressed against it; discomfort with light; unusually cold hands and feet; blue, grey, pale or blotchy skin or lips; extreme agitation (not stopping crying) or confusion; difficulty breathing (grunting noises, the stomach sucking under the ribcage), breathlessness or very fast breathing; or is not responding like they normally do, or is not interested in feeding or normal activities. A baby under 3 months with a temperature of 38°C or more, or 3–6 months with 39°C or more, needs urgent medical advice.

**Malaria is an emergency** and needs quick treatment with antimalarial medicines; you can die from it if it is not diagnosed and treated quickly (NHS). Anyone with a fever after being in a malaria area during the last 12 months should get medical help urgently. **Typhoid** is treated with antibiotics; a sudden stomach pain that keeps getting worse, black or dark red stools, or vomiting blood or material that looks like ground coffee are reasons to call emergency services (NHS).

## What is a fever?

According to MedlinePlus (U.S. National Library of Medicine), fever is the temporary increase in the body's temperature in response to a disease or illness. It is an important part of the body's defense against infection. The NHS (UK National Health Service) treats 38°C or more as a high temperature in children; fever is very common in young children and usually returns to normal within 1 to 4 days.

MedlinePlus notes that a simple cold or other viral infection can sometimes cause a high fever (38.9–40°C) without meaning there is a serious problem, while some serious infections do not cause a fever at all. A mild fever with no other problems does not need treatment — drink fluids and rest. Adults should contact a doctor for a fever lasting longer than 48–72 hours, a fever that stays at or above 39.4°C, fevers that come and go for a week or more, or a fever with a new rash, pain when urinating, or after recent travel abroad (MedlinePlus).

## Febrile seizures in children

A febrile seizure can sometimes happen when a child has a high temperature, usually between 6 months and 6 years old, and usually lasts 2 to 3 minutes (NHS). The child may become stiff, twitch or jerk, become unconscious, not respond, and be sleepy or irritable for up to 1 hour afterwards. After a first febrile seizure, the child needs to be checked in hospital (NHS).

On consequences, MedlinePlus says simple febrile seizures are harmless, and there is no evidence that they cause death, brain damage, epilepsy or learning problems. The NHS likewise says a febrile seizure is unlikely to harm a child or cause long-term effects. Most children outgrow febrile seizures by age 5 (MedlinePlus). Children who have had a febrile seizure have a slightly increased chance of epilepsy later in life, but this is rare (NHS). In infants and young children with a first seizure, doctors need to rule out other causes, especially meningitis (MedlinePlus) — which is why the child must be checked even after the seizure has stopped.

## Malaria and typhoid

**Malaria** is a serious infection spread by the bite of an infected mosquito, found in tropical regions including large areas of Africa and Asia (NHS). Symptoms include a high temperature or feeling hot, cold or shivery; sweating; headache and confusion; feeling very tired and sleepy (especially in children); feeling or being sick, tummy pain and diarrhoea; and jaundice. Symptoms usually appear 7 to 18 days after the bite (NHS). Malaria is treated with antimalarial medicines; complications can include brain infection, destruction of red blood cells, kidney failure, liver failure and respiratory failure (MedlinePlus).

**Typhoid fever** is an infection spread through unclean food or water (NHS). Symptoms include a high temperature that may slowly get worse over a few days, headache, cough, chills, tiredness, constipation and loss of appetite; tummy ache and diarrhoea usually come later. It is treated with antibiotics, and the full course must be finished even if you feel better (NHS). Untreated, typhoid can lead to internal bleeding or peritonitis (NHS); complications MedlinePlus lists also include intestinal perforation and kidney failure.

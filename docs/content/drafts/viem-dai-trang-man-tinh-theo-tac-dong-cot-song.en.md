## ⚠️ Signs that need urgent care

**Call emergency services or go to A&E right away** if (NHS): you have severe tummy pain; you are bleeding non-stop from your bottom; there is a lot of blood or you see large blood clots when you poo; or you are vomiting blood or poo, or your sick looks like coffee grounds. Do not drive yourself.

**Get an urgent same-day appointment** if (NHS): you have bloody or very bad diarrhoea; your poo is black or dark red; you have bowel symptoms with a high temperature (or feel hot, cold or shivery), a fast heartbeat, or you cannot fart or poo; you have lost a lot of weight for no reason; you find a hard lump or swelling in your tummy; or you have shortness of breath, noticeable heartbeats and skin paler than usual. Diarrhoea for more than 7 days, vomiting for more than 2 days, or being sick so often you cannot keep fluids down also need medical advice now.

The NHS advises seeing a doctor about changes in your poo that are not usual for you — softer poo, diarrhoea or constipation, pooing more or less often — blood in your poo, or ongoing tummy pain or bloating: these can also be symptoms of bowel cancer, and finding it early can make it easier to treat. Crohn's disease symptoms resemble many other bowel conditions; the NHS advises **not to try to diagnose the problem yourself**.

The document below groups these symptoms under chronic colitis. The quoted passages are not a diagnosis and do not replace an examination.

## Ulcerative colitis and Crohn's disease

According to the U.S. National Institute of Diabetes and Digestive and Kidney Diseases (NIDDK), **ulcerative colitis** is a chronic inflammatory bowel disease in which abnormal reactions of the immune system cause inflammation and ulcers on the inner lining of the large intestine. It can develop at any age but is more likely between 15 and 30. Experts are not sure what causes it but think genes, abnormal immune reactions, the microbiome and the environment play a role. Doctors diagnose it from symptoms, medical history, a physical exam and tests such as blood tests, stool tests and endoscopy of the large intestine. It is typically treated with medicines that reduce inflammation to bring on and maintain remission; some people need surgery.

The NHS (UK National Health Service) lists the common symptoms of ulcerative colitis: diarrhoea that may contain blood, mucus or pus; tummy pain, particularly in the lower left, which may get better after pooing; needing to poo frequently, urgently or at night; bleeding from the bottom; and wind or bloating. Periods with few or no symptoms alternate with flare-ups, which may bring a high temperature, joint pain, mouth ulcers or red eyes, and can be triggered by stress, infections, changes in medicines, NSAID painkillers such as ibuprofen or aspirin, or certain foods.

**Crohn's disease** is a long-term condition in which part of the gut becomes inflamed (NHS). Symptoms depend on the part affected: diarrhoea, blood or mucus in the poo, stomach pain, constipation, pain in the bottom, feeling very tired, loss of appetite and weight loss; children may not grow as expected. It cannot currently be cured, but there are treatments that help prevent or manage symptoms, under the care of a specialist in digestive conditions.

## Irritable bowel syndrome

The NHS says Crohn's disease symptoms can be similar to those of other gut conditions such as **irritable bowel syndrome (IBS)**, ulcerative colitis, diverticular disease and coeliac disease — which is why a doctor needs to tell them apart.

The main symptoms of IBS according to the NHS are stomach pain or cramps, usually worse after eating and better after pooing; bloating; diarrhoea, sometimes with a sudden need to poo; and constipation, straining and feeling you cannot empty your bowels fully. There may also be mucus in the poo, wind, tiredness, nausea and backache. Symptoms come and go, sometimes for no obvious reason and sometimes triggered by alcohol, caffeine, spicy or fatty food, stress and anxiety, or regular antibiotic use. The NHS advises seeing a doctor if you think you might have IBS and have had symptoms for over 4 weeks, so tests can rule out other problems.

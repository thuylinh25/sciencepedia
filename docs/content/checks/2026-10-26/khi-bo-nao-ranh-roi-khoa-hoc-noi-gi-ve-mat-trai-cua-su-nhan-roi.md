### D11 — điền `contentEn` (fill) — toàn bài, gồm "Further reading"

```markdown
Rest is an essential human need. But the brain does not truly "switch off" when we stop working: even at ease, many neural networks keep running. This article looks at what happens in the brain when we are idle, why that state is sometimes accompanied by anxiety in some people, and how to rest in a healthy way.

## The brain's default mode network

When we are not focused on a specific task, the brain does not stop working but shifts to a system called the **default mode network** (*DMN*). The neuroscientist Marcus Raichle and colleagues found that this set of cortical regions **increases** its activity when we rest and turn inward, and **decreases** it when we focus on a task that demands outward attention.

The DMN is involved in:

- Recalling memories.
- Thinking about oneself.
- Imagining the future.
- Processing social situations.

This is a perfectly normal part of brain activity and plays an important role in learning, creativity and self-awareness.

In some people, DMN activity is also **associated** with a tendency toward mind-wandering or repetitive thinking — the topic of the next section.

## Why can idleness make us anxious?

When there is no clear goal or task, the mind tends to drift (*mind-wandering*). A 2010 study in the journal *Science* tracked the daily activity of thousands of people and found that when the mind wandered away from the task at hand, people tended to be less happy than when they were focused — even when they were thinking about something pleasant.

The content the brain generates during such moments often includes:

- Memories of the past.
- Mistakes once made.
- Situations that have not yet happened.
- Latent worries.

When it repeats, this kind of thinking is called *rumination*. A 2020 meta-analysis in *NeuroImage* reported that rumination is **associated** with activity in the default mode network, and that rumination itself is linked to depression. This is an observed association; it does not establish that idleness "causes" anxiety. Even so, in some people, if this state persists it may come with:

- Excessive anxiety.
- Repetitive thinking.
- Psychological stress.
- A reduced sense of life satisfaction.

## The brain works on a "use it or lose it" principle

The brain can change its structure and neural connections throughout life, a phenomenon called **neuroplasticity**. The principles of experience-dependent plasticity (Kleim and Jones, 2008) describe how neural circuits that are used often are strengthened, while those used little may weaken — the principle often called "use it or lose it".

When we regularly:

- Learn.
- Solve problems.
- Interact socially.
- Encounter new experiences.

neural connections are strengthened and expanded.

## The impact of a sedentary lifestyle

Mental idleness often goes hand in hand with physical inactivity. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), [regular physical activity](/articles/van-dong-thay-doi-tim-va-mach-mau-nhu-the-nao) benefits brain health, helps reduce symptoms of anxiety and depression, improves cognition and lowers the risk of cognitive decline. The WHO recommends that adults do 150–300 minutes of moderate-intensity activity per week.

Conversely, prolonged sedentary behaviour (sitting or lying down too much) is linked to higher risks to cardiovascular and cognitive health over time. Physical activity is also a way to [relieve stress](/articles/stress-tac-dong-len-co-the-nhu-the-nao-va-vi-sao-van-dong-giup-chung-ta-giai-toa).

What matters is not being busy all the time, but maintaining an appropriate level of physical and mental activity.

## Does more free time mean more happiness?

Not necessarily. A 2021 study in the *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology* (Sharif, Mogilner and Hershfield) analysed data from thousands of people and found that both extremes are linked to lower satisfaction: too little free time comes with stress and overload, while too much free time comes with a feeling of lacking purpose. Satisfaction tends to be highest somewhere in between.

What matters is not only the amount of free time, but also how we use it — free time spent on meaningful activity is generally linked to higher satisfaction.

## What to do to rest the brain in a healthy way?

Effective rest does not mean stopping all activity.

Some activities considered good for the brain include:

- Reading books.
- Playing a musical instrument.
- Learning new skills.
- Walking.
- Gardening.
- Keeping a journal.
- Talking with friends.
- Meditation or mindfulness practice.
- [Getting enough sleep](/articles/giac-ngu-sau-lam-gi-voi-tri-nho-cua-ban).

These activities both help the brain relax and provide positive stimulation for the nervous system.

## When to see a doctor

Occasional anxiety or mind-wandering is normal. But according to the UK's National Health Service (NHS), you should see a doctor if:

- Anxiety affects your daily life and is hard to control.
- You feel anxious almost all the time for several weeks, lasting six months or more.
- You have had treatment but do not feel any better.

Seek urgent help immediately if you or someone you know is in a mental health crisis, or has thoughts of self-harm.

## Conclusion

The brain does not truly "switch off" when we do nothing. Even in a resting state, many neural networks keep working to process memories, thoughts and predictions about the future.

So the question is not whether we have free time, but how we use that time. A brain that is rested appropriately, physically active and continually learning tends to maintain better mental and cognitive health in the long run.
```

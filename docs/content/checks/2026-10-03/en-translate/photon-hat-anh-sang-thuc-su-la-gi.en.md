## What is a photon made of?

According to the Standard Model of particle physics, the photon is an **elementary particle**, meaning that no smaller internal structure has been found inside it.

A photon has no:

- Core or shell.
- Electric charge.
- Rest mass.

Even so, a photon still carries **energy and momentum** and travels through a vacuum at the speed of light:

**c ≈ 300,000 km/s**

A photon is not a tiny "marble of light". It is a quantum entity and the **quantum of the electromagnetic field**.

## The energy of a photon

A photon's energy depends on its frequency:

**E = h × f**

Where:

- **E** is the photon energy.
- **h** is Planck's constant.
- **f** is the frequency.

So:

**The higher the frequency → the more energetic the photon**

**The shorter the wavelength → the more energetic the photon**

A gamma-ray photon therefore carries far more energy than a radio-wave photon.

## Does a photon have a size?

A photon does not have a definite radius or diameter the way a ball does.

In the Standard Model, the photon is treated as a point-like elementary particle, but its quantum state can be spread out in space as a **wave packet**.

Wavelength is not the size of a photon. For example, red light having a wavelength of about 700 nm does not mean that a red photon is 700 nm wide.

## How do photons interact with matter?

When they meet matter, photons can be **absorbed, scattered or transmitted**.

### Absorption

In the **photoelectric effect**, a photon with enough energy can be absorbed and transfer its energy to an electron.

Part of the energy frees the electron from the material, and the rest becomes kinetic energy:

**Photon energy = Energy to free the electron + Kinetic energy of the electron**

### Scattering

In **Compton scattering**, a photon transfers part of its energy and momentum to an electron.

Afterwards:

- The electron gains energy.
- The photon changes direction.
- The remaining photon has lower energy and a longer wavelength.

### Transmission

If the probability of interacting with a material is low, the photon can pass straight through.

This is part of the reason glass lets [[anh-sang-kha-kien|visible light]] through but absorbs some ultraviolet and infrared wavelengths.

## Is a photon a wave or a particle?

Light shows wave properties through:

- Interference.
- Diffraction.
- Polarisation.

But the photoelectric effect and Compton scattering show that light exchanges energy in individual quanta.

That does not mean a photon is sometimes a wave and then turns into a particle.

**A photon is a quantum entity; "wave" and "particle" are two classical ways of describing different aspects of its behaviour.**

## The photon is also a "force carrier"

In the Standard Model, the photon is the **gauge boson of the electromagnetic interaction**.

In quantum electrodynamics (QED), the interaction between charged particles can be described through the exchange of **virtual photons**.

For example, the repulsion between two electrons can be calculated using processes in which virtual photons are exchanged.

Virtual photons are not ordinary light photons flying freely through space; they are components that appear in the quantum description of the electromagnetic interaction.

## Photons let us see and generate electricity

When photons reflect off an object and enter the eye, the [[vong-mac|retina]] turns the light information into nerve signals from which the brain builds an image.

In a solar cell, photons are absorbed by a semiconductor and transfer their energy to electrons, helping to produce an electric current.

Photons are also the "messengers" that let astronomers study the universe. Analysing photons from stars and galaxies can reveal their temperature, chemical composition, motion and many other properties.

💡 **As far as we currently know, a photon is not made of smaller particles. It is an elementary particle, the quantum of the electromagnetic field, which both carries energy and momentum and plays a central role in the electromagnetic interaction.**

From the light that lets us see to photons that have travelled for billions of years from distant galaxies, photons are among the most important "messengers" helping humanity explore the quantum world and the universe.

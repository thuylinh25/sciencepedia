## What are cosmic rays, really?

Primary cosmic rays are made up of many types of particle:

- [[proton|Protons]].
- Helium nuclei.
- Nuclei of carbon, oxygen, iron and many other elements.
- A share of electrons and other particles.

The heavy nuclei have lost their electrons, so they carry a positive charge.

Because they are charged, the paths of cosmic rays are bent by magnetic fields in the Milky Way. This is what makes tracing them back to their original sources so difficult. 

## Where do cosmic rays come from?

The source depends on the energy level.

### The Sun

Flares and eruptions on the Sun can release protons along with many high-energy ions.

This group is more precisely called **solar energetic particles**.

### Supernova remnants

Shock waves from exploding stars are considered one of the important mechanisms that can accelerate cosmic rays in the Milky Way to very high energies.

### Sources beyond the Milky Way

The origin of the highest-energy cosmic rays is still under investigation.

Candidates include extreme astronomical environments such as the regions around **active galactic nuclei**, where supermassive black holes and strong magnetic fields may take part in accelerating particles.

However, not every cosmic ray has been traced precisely back to its source. 

## What happens when cosmic rays hit the atmosphere?

High-energy cosmic rays rarely reach the ground intact.

When a primary particle strikes a nitrogen or oxygen nucleus high in the atmosphere, it creates many new particles.

The new particles go on colliding and decaying, creating a chain reaction called:

**An air shower**

Such a shower can contain:

- Pions.
- Kaons.
- Muons.
- Electrons.
- Positrons.
- [[photon|Photons]].
- Neutrons.

The energy of the original cosmic particle is thus split into a cascade of secondary particles spreading through the atmosphere.

## Muons: the visitors that actually reach the ground

One of the most notable products of air showers is the **muon**.

Muons are like electrons in that they carry one elementary charge (at ground level there are both negative muons and their antiparticles, positive muons), but they are much more massive.

They penetrate matter quite well, so some of the muons produced high in the atmosphere can travel all the way down to the ground.

The typical flux at ground level is about:

**1 muon/cm²/minute**

That means muons of cosmic-ray origin are constantly passing through our bodies without our feeling them at all.

## How does Earth protect us?

### Earth's magnetic field

Because most cosmic rays are charged, Earth's magnetic field can change the trajectories of many particles before they penetrate deep into the atmosphere.

### The atmosphere

The atmosphere is an especially important protective layer.

Instead of letting high-energy primary particles travel straight down to the ground, the atmosphere disperses their energy through a series of interactions that create secondary particles.

As a result, radiation exposure at ground level is far lower than in space. NASA uses dedicated models to calculate both primary cosmic rays and the secondary radiation produced as they pass through the atmosphere and shielding materials.

## Are cosmic rays dangerous to humans?

At ground level, the atmosphere provides a great deal of shielding, and cosmic rays contribute only part of the natural ionising radiation that humans receive.

But the higher you go, the thinner the atmosphere above you.

So cosmic rays become more important for:

- Astronauts.
- Spacecraft.
- Satellites.
- Crews and passengers on high-altitude flights.

NASA regards galactic cosmic rays and solar energetic particles as sources that must be accounted for when assessing radiation doses in aviation and spaceflight.

## Can cosmic rays cause computer errors?

Yes.

High-energy particles, or the secondary particles they create, can interact with semiconductor components.

A strong enough interaction can cause a **Single Event Effect (SEE)**, such as changing the state of a memory cell.

Put simply:

**0 → 1**

or:

**1 → 0**

without the software deliberately changing the data.

This is why electronic systems used in:

- Satellites.
- Spacecraft.
- Aviation.

must be carefully designed and assessed for radiation tolerance. NASA's NAIRAS model also calculates radiation fluxes to support the assessment of single-event effects on aviation and space microelectronics.

## How powerful can cosmic rays be?

Some cosmic rays reach energies far beyond the energy of individual particles in human-built accelerators.

Air-shower studies can now examine primary particles exceeding **10²⁰ electronvolts (eV)**. At these extreme energies, their origin and acceleration mechanisms still raise many unanswered questions. 

## Conclusion

🌌 **Cosmic rays are not a kind of light but mostly high-energy particles and atomic nuclei hurtling through space.**

When they strike Earth's atmosphere, their energy is turned into enormous showers of secondary particles. Some of these particles, especially muons, can reach the ground and pass through our bodies.

From exploding stars to extreme environments around distant objects, cosmic rays carry information about the most energetic processes in nature. So they are not only a source of radiation that must be managed in spaceflight, but also **"particle messengers" that help humanity study the most violent regions of the universe**.

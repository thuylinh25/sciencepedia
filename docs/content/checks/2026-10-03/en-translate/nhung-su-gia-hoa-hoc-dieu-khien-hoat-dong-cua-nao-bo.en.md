## How do neurotransmitters work?

When an electrical impulse reaches the end of a neuron, small vesicles containing [[chat-dan-truyen-than-kinh|neurotransmitters]] release them into the synaptic cleft.

These molecules:

1. Cross the synaptic cleft.
2. Bind to receptors on the target cell.
3. Change that cell's activity.
4. Are then broken down, taken back up or cleared from the synapse.

Depending on the substance and the receptor, the signal can make the target cell more likely to fire, less likely to fire, or change the way it responds to other signals.

## Dopamine: motivation, reward and movement

[[Dopamine]] is famous for its role in the reward system, but simply calling it the "happiness hormone" is inaccurate.

Dopamine is involved in:

- The drive to seek rewards.
- Learning from rewards.
- Predicting outcomes.
- Attention.
- Controlling movement.

Dopamine is especially important in making us want to repeat behaviours that have previously led to beneficial outcomes.

## Serotonin: regulating many functions

[[Serotonin]] helps regulate:

- Mood.
- Sleep.
- Appetite.
- Digestion.
- Some cognitive processes.

However, depression cannot be explained simply by the statement:

**"A lack of serotonin causes depression."**

Mental disorders usually involve a complex network of many neurotransmitters, [[gen|genes]], the environment and the activity of neural circuits.

## GABA: the brain's "brake"

**GABA (gamma-aminobutyric acid)** is an important inhibitory neurotransmitter of the central nervous system.

It reduces neurons' ability to fire signals, helping to prevent the nervous system from becoming overexcited. 

You can picture it as:

**Glutamate = the accelerator**

**GABA = the brake**

A healthy brain needs the two to work together; it is not a case of the more GABA or dopamine the better.

## Glutamate: the brain's key "accelerator"

Glutamate is one of the brain's most important excitatory neurotransmitters.

It is heavily involved in:

- Learning.
- Memory.
- Neuroplasticity.
- Signalling between neurons.

In particular, glutamate receptors such as NMDA play an important role in the synaptic changes associated with learning and memory. 

## Adrenaline: preparing the body for action

Adrenaline, or epinephrine, can act as a neurotransmitter in some contexts and is also a hormone when released into the blood by the adrenal glands.

In stressful situations, adrenaline contributes to the **"fight or flight"** response:

- The heart beats faster.
- The airways open wider.
- Blood is directed preferentially to the organs needed for action.

This lets the body respond quickly to danger.

## Oxytocin: more than a "love hormone"

Oxytocin is a neuropeptide and hormone with important roles in:

- Labour.
- Milk release.
- Social behaviour.
- Some bonding processes between individuals.

However, simply calling oxytocin the **"love hormone"** is misleading, because its effects vary with the context and the neural systems involved.

## Endorphins: the body's own painkilling system

Endorphins belong to the group of opioid peptides that the body makes itself.

They can:

- Reduce the sensation of pain.
- Adjust the response to stress.
- Contribute to the pleasant feeling after some kinds of physical activity.

They are part of the body's natural pain-relief system.

## Can you "hack" your neurotransmitters?

There is no simple way to control mood by boosting dopamine, serotonin or GABA on their own.

The brain is a complex network in which many chemical systems work at the same time.

Habits that benefit brain function in general include:

- Getting enough sleep on a regular schedule.
- Regular physical activity.
- A varied diet.
- Maintaining social relationships.
- Limiting alcohol and addictive substances.
- Managing stress.

These activities affect many neural systems at once, rather than flipping a single "dopamine switch" or "serotonin switch".

## The brain is not run by 7 chemicals

Scientists know of **dozens of neurotransmitters**, and each can produce different effects depending on the type of receptor and its location in the body. 

But emotions do not work according to simple equations such as:

**Dopamine = fun**

**Serotonin = happiness**

**GABA = calm**

In reality, an emotion or behaviour is the result of coordinated activity among many neural networks, neurotransmitters, hormones, memories and signals from the body.

🧠 Neurotransmitters are not "emotion buttons" but the chemical language that lets billions of neurons coordinate with one another. It is from that vast communication network that memory, movement, attention and the complex emotional states of human beings emerge.

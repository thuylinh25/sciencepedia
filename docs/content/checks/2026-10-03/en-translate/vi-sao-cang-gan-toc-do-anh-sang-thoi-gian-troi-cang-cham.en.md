## What is special about the speed of light?

In a vacuum, the speed of light is:

**c ≈ 300,000 km/s**

One of the foundations of [special relativity](/articles/thuyet-tuong-doi-hep-khi-thoi-gian-khong-con-tuyet-doi) is that all observers in inertial frames of reference measure the same speed of light in a vacuum, no matter how the light source is moving.

It is this that forces space and time to be relative.

## How does a light clock explain time slowing down?

Imagine a clock made of two mirrors with a [photon](/articles/photon-hat-anh-sang-thuc-su-la-gi) bouncing back and forth between them.

Each trip of the photon from one mirror to the other is used as one tick of time.

### When the clock is at rest

The photon travels straight between the two mirrors.

### When the clock moves very fast

For an outside observer, while the photon is travelling, the whole clock is moving too.

So the photon has to follow a longer, diagonal path to reach the other mirror.

But the speed of light must still be **c**.

Because the photon has to cover a longer distance at an unchanged speed, the time between two ticks, as measured by the outside observer, must be longer.

The result:

**A moving clock runs slow.**

Importantly, it is not just the clock that slows. Every physical process moving along with it is subject to the same [[gian-no-thoi-gian|time dilation]].

## The faster you go, the stronger the effect

The amount of time dilation is given by the **Lorentz factor**:

**γ = 1 / √(1 − v²/c²)**

Where:

- **v** is the relative velocity.
- **c** is the speed of light.
- **γ** tells you how much time is dilated.

For example:

| Velocity | Factor γ |
|---|---:|
| 50% c | ~1.15 |
| 90% c | ~2.29 |
| 99% c | ~7.09 |
| 99.9% c | ~22.4 |

At **99.9% of the speed of light**, if the people on board a spacecraft experience about 1 year, then about 22.4 years may pass in a suitable outside frame of reference.

This is the physical basis for the idea of **"travelling into the future"** at extremely high speed.

## So does time stop at the speed of light?

This is where it is very easy to go wrong.

When an object with mass approaches the speed of light:

**v → c**

then:

**γ → infinity**

The proper time along the path of an object moving close to the speed of light can become very small compared with the time measured in another frame of reference.

But you cannot simply put a person or a spacecraft into the case:

**v = c**

because no object with rest mass can reach the speed of light.

## Does a photon "feel time stand still"?

That is not the right way to put it.

A photon travels through a vacuum at the speed of light, and along a light path, **the proper time is zero**.

But from this you cannot conclude:

**"From the photon's point of view, everything happens instantly."**

The reason is that relativity **does not allow a frame of reference at rest with a photon to be constructed**.

A photon does not have its "own frame of reference" the way a person on a spacecraft does.

So a more accurate way to put it is:

**The proper time along the path of light is zero, but there is no valid physical point of view of the photon from which to ask how the photon experiences time.**

## Why can't objects with mass reach the speed of light?

The faster an object with rest mass is accelerated, the more energy it needs to keep increasing its speed.

As:

**v → c**

the energy required:

**E → infinity**

That is why, in theory, a spacecraft can get ever closer to the speed of light but cannot be accelerated to exactly **c** with a finite amount of energy.

There is no need to say that "the spacecraft's mass becomes infinite".

In the modern description, **an object's rest mass does not change**, while its energy and momentum increase very sharply as its speed approaches the speed of light.

## Could you exceed the speed of light and go back to the past?

Relativity does not allow an object with mass to simply accelerate:

**slower than light → equal to light → faster than light**

Taking the usual time-dilation formula and substituting **v > c** to conclude that "time becomes negative" is not a valid physical interpretation either.

Ideas about faster-than-light motion raise deep problems of **causality**, but there is currently no evidence that humans could accelerate an ordinary object past the light barrier.

## What about travelling to the future?

That, on the other hand, is entirely consistent with relativity.

If an astronaut made a journey at a speed extremely close to the speed of light and then returned to Earth, they could experience less time than the people on Earth.

The astronaut would not feel their own clock running slow.

Their heart would beat normally.

Their thoughts would be normal.

The clock on board would run normally.

Only when the two meet again and compare clocks would they see:

**They have lived through different amounts of time.**

## Conclusion

🚀 It is not that the universe "brakes the clock" when an object moves fast.

Time dilation is a consequence of the structure of spacetime and of the fact that the speed of light in a vacuum has the same value in every inertial frame of reference.

The closer you get to the speed of light:

**Speed increases → Lorentz factor increases → the time difference grows**

But objects with mass cannot reach the speed of light, and there is no "photon's point of view" from which to say that a photon sees the whole universe frozen.

The most accurate way to state the original question is:

**It is not that "time stops when you reach the speed of light"; rather, as the speed of an object with mass approaches the speed of light, its proper time becomes smaller and smaller compared with the time measured in a frame of reference moving relative to it.**

## From absolute time to relative time

In Newtonian physics, time is seen as an invisible clock ticking evenly throughout the universe.

Einstein completely changed that view.

In relativity:

**Space + time = spacetime**

Time does not pass the same way for everyone.

It can pass more slowly when:

- An object is [moving at very high speed](/articles/vi-sao-cang-gan-toc-do-anh-sang-thoi-gian-troi-cang-cham).
- An object is in a strong gravitational field.

This effect does not exist only in theory. Precise positioning systems such as GPS have to account for relativistic effects in order to work correctly.

## Quantum mechanics has a problem with time

In ordinary [quantum mechanics](/articles/co-hoc-luong-tu-the-gioi-ky-la-phia-sau-vat-chat), time is used as an external parameter to describe how the state of a system changes.

But in general relativity, time is part of spacetime itself.

When we try to quantise gravity, these two views collide.

A famous example appears in the **Wheeler–DeWitt equation**, an equation studied in quantum gravity.

Unlike the familiar Schrödinger equation, it does not contain an external time variable in the usual way.

This gives rise to:

**The "problem of time"**

## Could time be an "emergent" phenomenon?

An intriguing idea was proposed by Don Page and William Wootters in 1983.

Instead of needing a clock outside the universe, we can divide a quantum system into:

**A clock + the rest of the system**

The overall state may not change with respect to an external time, but the parts inside it can still change **relative to one another**.

For example:

When the clock shows state A → the system is in state X

When the clock shows state B → the system is in state Y

An observer inside the system will see:

**X → Y**

and interpret that change as time passing.

On this view, time may arise from **correlations between physical systems**, rather than needing to exist as an absolute clock of the universe.

However, this is still a line of theoretical research, not evidence that "time is just an illusion".

## Why does time only run forwards?

Another mystery is the **arrow of time**.

We:

- Remember the past but not the future.
- See a cup shatter but never see the shards reassemble into a cup.
- See people grow old but never grow young again.

Yet many of the fundamental equations of physics do not draw such a simple distinction between past and future.

An important answer comes from **[[entropy]]**.

According to the second law of thermodynamics, the entropy of a closed system tends to increase.

You can picture it as:

**An ordered state → a state with many more possible arrangements**

An egg breaks and gets mixed up far more easily than its yolk, white and shell reassemble themselves into an intact egg.

The increase in entropy creates a natural direction that we recognise as:

**Past → present → future**

## Atomic clocks: using quantum physics to measure time

Even though we do not know whether time is a fundamental quantity, we can measure it with astonishing precision thanks to quantum mechanics.

The second in the International System of Units is defined through the frequency of the hyperfine transition of the **caesium-133** atom.

The standard value is:

**9,192,631,770 Hz**

Identical atoms have extremely stable energy transitions, so they become "quantum pendulums" far more precise than ordinary mechanical motion.

## Optical clocks are even more precise

The new generation of atomic clocks uses optical transitions of atoms or ions.

Their precision has reached very high levels, allowing scientists to:

- Test relativity.
- Measure gravitational differences between heights.
- Improve the accuracy of positioning.
- Search for signs of new physics.

A precise enough clock can even detect that time passes differently between two places whose heights differ only very slightly.

In other words, quantum clocks can turn the "stretching of time" into a measurable quantity.

## So is time an illusion?

Science cannot yet answer with certainty.

There are at least three broad possibilities:

**1. Time is a fundamental component of nature.**

**2. Time exists but its nature is different from how we experience it.**

**3. Time is a phenomenon that emerges from deeper relationships between physical systems.**

The fact that the Wheeler–DeWitt equation does not contain time in the familiar way does not prove that time does not exist.

It shows something more interesting:

**The two most successful theories of modern physics still do not fully agree on the role of time.**

## Conclusion

⏳ Newton saw time as the universe's absolute clock.

Einstein showed that this clock can run fast or slow depending on motion and gravity.

And quantum physics and quantum gravity raise a deeper question:

**Could it be that, at the deepest level of reality, time does not exist in the way we have always assumed?**

We do not yet have a final answer.

But it is this very paradox that makes time one of the greatest remaining mysteries of modern physics: something we experience every second and can measure with extraordinary precision, yet whose true nature we still do not really know.

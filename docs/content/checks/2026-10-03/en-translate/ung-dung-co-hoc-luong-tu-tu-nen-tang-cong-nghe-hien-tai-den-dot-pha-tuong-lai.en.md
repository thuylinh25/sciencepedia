## Transistors and computer chips

The modern electronics revolution relies heavily on **the quantum physics of semiconductors**.

By understanding how electrons behave in materials such as silicon, humans were able to build **transistors**, tiny electronic switches used to process information.

Today, billions of transistors can be integrated on a single chip to run:

- Smartphones.
- Computers.
- Cars.
- Electronic devices.
- Data centres.

Without quantum physics, modern semiconductor technology would not exist in the form we know today.

## Lasers

Lasers rely on transitions between the quantum energy levels of atoms and on the phenomenon of **stimulated emission**.

The result is a beam of light that is highly directional and controllable.

Lasers are used in:

- Fibre-optic cables.
- Barcode scanners.
- Distance measurement.
- Industrial cutting and welding.
- Surgery and medical treatment.
- Scientific research.

## MRI

**Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)** exploits a quantum property called **nuclear spin**.

When hydrogen nuclei in the body are placed in a strong magnetic field and exposed to radio waves, they produce signals that a computer can process into images.

This lets doctors see many tissues inside the body in detail without using ionising radiation such as X-rays.

## Quantum computers

Ordinary computers use **bits**:

**0 or 1**

Quantum computers use **qubits**, which can exist in a superposition of 0 and 1.

Quantum algorithms exploit three special phenomena:

- **Superposition:** allows complex quantum states to be represented.
- **Entanglement:** creates quantum correlations between qubits.
- **Interference:** increases the probability of useful results and decreases the probability of unwanted ones.

Importantly, a quantum computer **does not simply try all the answers at once**.

Its power comes from controlling interference between quantum states to solve certain problems in a way that differs from classical computers.

## Will quantum computers replace today's computers?

Most likely not.

Quantum computers are not designed to run Word, watch films or browse the web faster than an ordinary laptop.

Their greatest potential lies in certain special problems, such as:

- Simulating quantum systems and molecules.
- Researching new materials.
- Some cryptographic problems.
- Some kinds of optimisation and specialised computation.

So in the future, quantum computers will most likely work **alongside classical computers**, rather than replacing them entirely.

## Quantum networks

Another developing field is **quantum networks**.

Their goals may include:

- Connecting quantum processors.
- Distributing entangled states between different locations.
- Building distributed quantum sensor systems.
- Transmitting cryptographic keys using quantum protocols.

### Quantum key distribution

One well-known application is **Quantum Key Distribution (QKD)**.

In some QKD protocols, an eavesdropper measuring the quantum states can create statistical changes that the two communicating parties can detect.

This provides a way to check whether a key exchange has been tampered with.

However:

**A quantum network does not mean an unhackable network.**

Computers, software, end devices and people can still become weak points in the overall system.

## Quantum sensors

Another important direction is **quantum sensors**.

By controlling extremely sensitive quantum states, these devices can measure very small changes in:

- Magnetic fields.
- [[gia-toc|Acceleration]].
- Gravity.
- Time.
- Other physical quantities.

[Atomic clocks](/articles/thoi-gian-duoi-goc-nhin-luong-tu-vu-tru-co-thuc-su-troi) are a prominent example of using quantum transitions to measure time with very high precision.

## Two quantum revolutions

The history of quantum technology can be pictured in two stages.

### The first quantum revolution

Humans learned to understand and exploit the quantum properties of matter.

The result:

**Transistors → chips → lasers → MRI → modern electronics**

### The second quantum revolution

Humans began to actively control individual quantum systems and phenomena such as:

- Superposition.
- Entanglement.
- Quantum interference.

The goal:

**Quantum computers → quantum networks → quantum sensors**

## Conclusion

⚛️ Quantum mechanics is not just a theory about the microscopic world.

It helped create **transistors, computer chips, lasers and MRI**, technologies that shape modern society.

Today, humanity is going a step further: instead of only exploiting the quantum properties of materials, we are beginning to actively **create, control and connect quantum states**.

If the first quantum revolution helped create the electronic age, the next one may open up entirely new ways to **compute, communicate and measure the world**.

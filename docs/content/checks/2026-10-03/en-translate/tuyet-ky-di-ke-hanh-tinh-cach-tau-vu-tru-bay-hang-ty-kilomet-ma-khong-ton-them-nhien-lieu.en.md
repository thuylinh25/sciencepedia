## Why do spacecraft usually travel along the ecliptic?

Most of the planets in the Solar System lie almost in the same plane, because they formed from a rotating disc of gas and dust about 4.57 billion years ago.

This plane is where the line of the [[hoang-dao|ecliptic]] lies on the celestial sphere.

Launching a spacecraft along the plane of the ecliptic:

- Saves a great deal of energy.
- Makes the other planets easy to reach.
- Avoids the extremely fuel-hungry manoeuvres needed to change orbital plane.

You can think of the ecliptic as the Solar System's "main highway".

## What is a gravity assist?

A planet does not just have mass; it is also moving around the Sun at very high speed.

For example:

- Earth: about 30 km/s.
- Jupiter: about 13 km/s.

When a spacecraft flies close past a planet, the planet's gravity bends the spacecraft's trajectory.

### Going the right way

The spacecraft will:

- Pick up momentum from the planet.
- Increase its speed relative to the Sun.
- Travel farther while using almost no propellant.

### Going the opposite way

The spacecraft can:

- Lose some momentum.
- Slow down.
- Fall deeper into the inner Solar System.

## Is it really "stealing" energy?

Physically, yes.

The spacecraft's extra energy comes from the planet's own orbital motion.

By the law of conservation of momentum:

- The spacecraft speeds up.
- The planet loses a corresponding amount of momentum.

However, the effect is extremely small.

For example:

- Jupiter is about 318 times as massive as Earth.
- A probe weighs only a few hundred kilograms or a few tonnes.

The change in the planet's orbit is so small that it cannot be measured in practice.

## Famous missions that used gravity assists

### Voyager 1 and Voyager 2

NASA took advantage of a rare alignment of:

- Jupiter
- Saturn
- Uranus
- Neptune

to "hitch a ride" past these planets one after another. Voyager 2 is the only spacecraft ever to have visited Uranus and Neptune.

As a result:

- Voyager 1 and Voyager 2 are the only two spacecraft ever to have operated beyond the heliosphere, in interstellar space.
- Their total distances travelled run to tens of billions of kilometres.

### Cassini

The Cassini spacecraft did not fly straight to Saturn.

It flew past, in turn:

- Venus
- Earth
- Jupiter

to build up enough speed before reaching its destination.

### Parker Solar Probe

The spacecraft's goal is to get close to the Sun.

That sounds as if it only needs to dive inward, but in reality the spacecraft has to shed most of its orbital velocity around the Sun.

NASA used multiple flybys of **Venus** to brake it gradually, allowing Parker to get closer to the Sun than any spacecraft in history.

## Why does this technique matter?

Without [[tro-giup-hap-dan|gravity assists]]:

- Many missions would need far larger rockets.
- The mass of propellant would rise sharply.
- Some missions would be practically impossible with current technology.

By "hitchhiking" on planets, humanity can send spacecraft to:

- The edge of the Solar System.
- Interstellar space.
- Planets billions of kilometres away.

with only a relatively limited amount of propellant.

## Conclusion

🚀 The gravity assist is one of the most elegant applications of celestial mechanics. Instead of fighting the laws of nature, scientists use the planets' own motion to turn them into giant "free accelerators". Thanks to this technique, spacecraft such as Voyager, Cassini and Parker Solar Probe have covered distances that would have been almost impossible on the propellant they carried alone.

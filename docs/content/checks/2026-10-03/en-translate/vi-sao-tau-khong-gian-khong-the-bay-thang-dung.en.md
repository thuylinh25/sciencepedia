## The biggest obstacle: Earth is already moving at 30 km/s

Earth is not standing still.

It orbits the Sun at a speed of about:

> **29.8 km/s (~108,000 km/h)**

Every rocket launched from Earth automatically inherits this speed.

To leave the Solar System in a direction perpendicular to the plane of the [[hoang-dao|ecliptic]], a spacecraft would have to:

1. Cancel almost all of its 29.8 km/s sideways velocity.
2. Then accelerate in the perpendicular direction.

That is an enormous change in the direction of velocity.

To picture it:

- Flying in the direction Earth is moving is like running along a conveyor belt that is already racing forward.
- Flying perpendicular is like having to stop dead on that conveyor belt before jumping off to the side.

In orbital mechanics, changing the plane of an orbit is one of the most fuel-hungry manoeuvres there is.

## Flying sideways is much easier

At Earth's orbital distance, the speed needed to escape the Sun's gravity is:

> **42.1 km/s**

A spacecraft launched in the direction of Earth's motion around the Sun already has:

> **29.8 km/s**

So it only needs an extra:

**42.1 − 29.8 = 12.3 km/s**

to escape the Solar System.

This is why almost every interplanetary probe sets off close to the plane of the ecliptic.

## Why does flying straight "up" take 51.6 km/s?

To fly fully perpendicular, the spacecraft must at the same time:

- Cancel the 29.8 km/s sideways velocity.
- Reach the 42.1 km/s escape velocity in the perpendicular direction.

These two velocities are at right angles, so they add by Pythagoras' theorem:

**Δv = √(29.8² + 42.1²)**

**Δv = √(888.04 + 1772.41)**

**Δv = √2660.45**

**Δv ≈ 51.6 km/s**

### Comparing the energy required

| Direction of flight | Delta-v required |
|------------|------------------|
| Along the ecliptic | ~12.3 km/s |
| Perpendicular to the ecliptic | ~51.6 km/s |

That is more than **4 times** larger.

The problem, however, is even more serious than that.

According to the Tsiolkovsky rocket equation, the amount of propellant needed grows **exponentially**, not linearly, with delta-v. So a mission requiring a delta-v of 51.6 km/s could need tens or hundreds of times more propellant than a mission that needs only 12.3 km/s.

That is beyond the capability of most of today's chemical rockets.

## No planets to "hitch a ride" on

Along the plane of the ecliptic lie:

- Venus
- Earth
- Mars
- Jupiter
- Saturn
- Uranus
- Neptune

These are the "free accelerators" for the technique known as:

> **[[tro-giup-hap-dan|Gravity assist]] (Gravity Assist / Slingshot)**

When a spacecraft flies close past a planet, it can:

- Speed up.
- Slow down.
- Change direction.
- Change its orbital plane.

while using almost no propellant.

If it flies perpendicular:

> There are almost no planets to pass by.

That means every change in velocity has to be made by the spacecraft's own engines.

## Few scientific targets

Most of the matter in the Solar System is concentrated near the plane of the ecliptic:

- All the major planets.
- The asteroid belt.
- The Kuiper Belt.
- Most short-period comets.

Flying perpendicular, a spacecraft would quickly leave the busiest part of the Solar System and head into almost empty space.

So the scientific return is usually much lower than on journeys along the ecliptic.

## A famous exception: Ulysses

NASA did once fly a mission that went almost "perpendicular" in order to study the Sun's poles.

That spacecraft was:

> **Ulysses (1990)**

But it was not launched straight out of the plane of the ecliptic.

Instead:

1. It was launched along the ecliptic towards Jupiter.
2. It flew extremely close past that planet.
3. Jupiter's enormous gravity was used to bend its orbit to nearly perpendicular to the ecliptic.

In other words:

> To get off the "ecliptic highway", scientists still had to use a planet sitting on that very highway as a springboard.

## Conclusion

🚀 In theory, a spacecraft can certainly fly perpendicular to the plane of the ecliptic.

But to do so, it would have to:

- Cancel nearly 30 km/s of Earth's orbital velocity.
- Supply a delta-v of about 51.6 km/s on its own.
- Go without gravity assists from the planets.
- Have very few scientific targets to visit.

That is why almost every interplanetary mission chooses to stay on the "ecliptic highway", where the planets are both destinations and free gravitational boost stations that let humanity explore the Solar System at the lowest possible cost.

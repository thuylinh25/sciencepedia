## 2°C globally is not like a day that's 2°C hotter

The figures of 1.5°C and 2°C are increases in **global average temperature relative to the pre-industrial period**, not the temperature in any particular city.

They are calculated from temperatures over:

- The continents.
- The oceans.
- Many different regions of the planet.
- Long periods of time.

So a small change in the global average can bring large changes in individual regions.

When the average temperature rises, the whole temperature distribution shifts. Days once considered unusually hot become more frequent, and extreme heat events can become more intense.

## How different are 1.5°C and 2°C?

The difference is only:

**0.5°C**

but the impacts do not increase in a simple way.

At higher levels of warming, extreme climate events generally become more frequent or more severe.

### Heatwaves

With each additional increment of warming, extreme heat events become more frequent and more intense.

A heatwave that used to occur on average about once every 10 years in a climate with little human influence is projected to occur much more often as global temperature rises.

### The Arctic Ocean

At 1.5°C of warming, the Arctic Ocean is projected to have a summer almost free of sea ice about **once per century**.

At 2°C:

**At least once per decade.**

That does not mean the Arctic disappears entirely, but the difference in frequency is very large.

### Coral reefs

These are among the ecosystems most sensitive to temperature.

At 1.5°C, tropical coral reefs are projected to decline by a further:

**70–90%**

At 2°C:

**More than 99%**

This is one of the clearest examples of how an extra half degree of warming can make a very large difference to an ecosystem.

### Sea level

Limiting warming to 1.5°C rather than 2°C could reduce global sea-level rise by 2100 by about:

**10 cm**

That sounds small, but on low-lying, densely populated coastlines, 10 cm can significantly change the number of people exposed to flooding and rising seas.

## Why does extreme weather become more dangerous as it gets hotter?

Warmer air can hold more water vapour.

This changes Earth's water cycle.

In some regions:

- Extreme rainfall can become heavier.
- Flood risk increases.

While in other regions:

- Soils dry out faster.
- Droughts become more severe.
- Wildfire risk rises.

So climate change does not simply mean:

**"Everywhere gets 2°C hotter."**

It means **the whole climate system is changing**, affecting temperature, rainfall, drought, snow and ice, and the oceans.

## Feedbacks: when warming amplifies itself

An especially important factor is **climate feedbacks**.

### Melting ice makes Earth absorb more heat

Ice and snow are bright, so they reflect a large share of sunlight back into space.

When the ice melts:

**Bright surface → darker ocean or land surface**

A darker surface absorbs more solar energy.

The result:

**Warming → ice melts → more heat absorbed → stronger warming**

This is a **positive feedback**, a process that amplifies the initial change.

## Permafrost is also a problem

Permafrost regions in the Arctic store very large amounts of carbon in frozen organic matter.

As the climate warms, some of this ground thaws. Microbes can break down the organic matter and release:

- Carbon dioxide.
- Methane.

Both are [[khi-nha-kinh|greenhouse gases]].

This creates another feedback:

**Warming → permafrost thaws → more greenhouse gas emissions → more warming**

However, you should not imagine all the methane suddenly escaping as a "climate bomb". The process is complex and varies from region to region.

## What is a tipping point?

Some components of the Earth system may have **tipping points**.

The idea is that when a system is pushed too far from its original state, it may begin to change in ways that:

- Are hard to reverse.
- Last for centuries or longer.
- Continue even after the original cause has diminished.

Systems under study include:

- The great ice sheets of Greenland and Antarctica.
- Permafrost.
- Some large ecosystems.
- Ocean circulation systems.

There is no single temperature at which all tipping points are triggered at once. But **the risk increases as global temperature keeps rising**.

## Why do the oceans matter so much?

Most of the excess heat from climate change is absorbed by the oceans.

As the oceans warm:

- Seawater expands.
- Sea level rises.
- Marine heatwaves become stronger.
- Marine ecosystems come under pressure.
- Land ice keeps melting and adding water to the oceans.

Sea level has another worrying feature:

**It responds very slowly.**

Even once temperatures are finally stabilised, some changes in the oceans and ice sheets may continue for centuries.

## 1.5°C is not "safe", and 2°C is not the end of the world

This is the point most easily misunderstood.

There is no climate cliff at:

**1.49°C = safe**

**1.50°C = catastrophe**

Likewise, exceeding 2°C does not mean civilisation instantly collapses.

Instead, risk increases progressively with temperature:

**1.5°C is more dangerous than 1°C**

**2°C is more dangerous than 1.5°C**

**3°C is more dangerous than 2°C**

So even if a temperature target is exceeded, continuing to limit every tenth of a degree of warming still matters.

## Conclusion

🌍 One or two degrees sounds tiny when talking about a day's weather, but it is an enormous change when talking about **the average temperature of the entire planet**.

Each additional fraction of a degree can mean:

- Stronger heatwaves.
- Changes in extreme rainfall and drought.
- More melting ice.
- Higher sea levels.
- Greater losses for coral reefs and ecosystems.
- A rising risk of crossing tipping points.

So the question is not only:

**"Can we stay below exactly 1.5°C?"**

The more important question is:

**"How much of the further warming can we prevent?"**

Because in climate, **every tenth of a degree matters**.

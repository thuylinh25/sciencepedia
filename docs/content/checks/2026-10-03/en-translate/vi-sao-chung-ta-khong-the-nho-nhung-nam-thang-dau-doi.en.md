## Young children really do have memory

Infantile amnesia does not mean that babies cannot remember.

Children can learn:

- Familiar faces.
- Voices.
- Words.
- Places.
- Routines.
- Motor skills.

The problem lies mainly in one particular kind of memory:

**Autobiographical memory**, the ability to recall, many years later, a specific event that happened to you.

This is a system that needs many components of the brain and cognition working together.

## The hippocampus is still maturing

One brain region especially important for forming memories of events is the **[[hoi-hai-ma|hippocampus]]**.

In the first years of life, the hippocampus and its related networks are still developing rapidly.

So a young child's brain is very good at learning, but the system that keeps and retrieves autobiographical memories over many years does not yet work like an adult's.

Research also suggests that **neurogenesis**, the formation of new neurons in the hippocampus, may play a role.

## Could too many new neurons make memories hard to retrieve?

Early in life, the hippocampus has very high rates of new neuron formation and network reorganisation.

One hypothesis holds that as new neurons are continually integrated into hippocampal networks, the circuits that once helped store memories are also altered.

You can picture it like this:

**A library that still has its books but keeps rebuilding its shelving and changing its numbering system.**

The book may not have disappeared completely, but years later finding it again becomes very difficult.

Animal experiments show that increased neurogenesis can make previously formed memories harder to retrieve.

However, this is only **part of the explanation**, not the sole cause of infantile amnesia.

## Language helps memories last longer

As adults, we do not store events only as images.

We turn them into stories:

**"It was raining that day. I went to the zoo with my parents and saw an elephant for the first time."**

Language helps us:

- Name things.
- Put events in order.
- Link causes with effects.
- Retell memories many times.

Very young children do not yet have fully developed language and storytelling.

As their language develops, children also gradually become able to build more structured autobiographical memories.

But language cannot be the whole answer, because infantile amnesia also occurs in animals that do not have language as humans do.

## A "self" also has to form

To create a memory of the kind:

**"This happened to me"**

the brain needs a fairly stable concept of self.

Awareness of the "self" develops gradually during the first years of life.

Around the second year, many children start to show that they can recognise themselves in a mirror.

Gradually, children come to understand:

**I am an individual → I exist over time → this event happened to me**

This is one of the foundations of autobiographical memory.

## Do early memories really disappear?

This is the most intriguing question.

Some animal studies suggest that memories formed very early may **not be completely erased**, but instead become hard to retrieve by ordinary means in adulthood.

This raises the possibility that infantile amnesia may be, at least in part:

**A problem of memory retrieval**

rather than simply:

**Memories being wiped clean.**

In humans, the question of what form early memories survive in is still being researched.

## If we don't remember, does early childhood still affect us?

There is one very important point:

**Not remembering an experience does not mean that experience had no effect on the brain.**

The first years of life are a period in which children are constantly learning:

- Language.
- Movement.
- Recognising people.
- Responding to their environment.
- Basic social rules.

Most of us do not remember each time we learned these things, but the results of that learning remain.

More caution is needed with claims that a specific experience we do not remember definitely shaped a particular personality trait, fear or preference in adulthood. Human development is influenced by a great many different factors.

## Conclusion

🧠 We forget our earliest years not because young children have no memory.

**Infantile amnesia is most likely the result of several processes happening at once:**

- The hippocampus is still maturing.
- Neural networks are changing very rapidly.
- High neurogenesis may make older memories hard to retrieve.
- Language and storytelling are not yet fully developed.
- The concept of self is still forming.

So the first years of life are not a "blank" for the brain.

**We learned a great deal in those years. What is special is only that, as adults, we can no longer easily open our own diary and read its first pages.**

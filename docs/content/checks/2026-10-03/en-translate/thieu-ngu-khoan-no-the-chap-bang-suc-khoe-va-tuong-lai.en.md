## What does the brain do while we sleep?

Sleep is not a time when the brain "switches off".

During sleep, the brain still carries out many important tasks:

- Consolidating memories.
- Regulating emotions.
- Restoring neural function.
- Regulating metabolism.
- Helping to clear some metabolic by-products.

One much-studied system is the **glymphatic system**, a fluid-transport network involved in clearing waste from brain tissue. Its activity is closely linked to sleep, especially deep NREM sleep.

## Sleep loss and "waste" in the brain

One substance of interest is **beta-amyloid**, a [[protein]] associated with Alzheimer's disease when it builds up abnormally in the brain.

Studies show that sleep loss can change the amount of beta-amyloid measured in some brain regions.

However, this does not mean:

**One night of sleep loss → causes Alzheimer's**

The link between sleep and Alzheimer's is much more complex. Long-term poor sleep may affect processes related to the disease, while the disease-related changes in the brain can themselves disrupt sleep.

## The brain works less well when short of sleep

One of the most noticeable consequences of sleep deprivation is impaired cognition.

You may experience:

- Difficulty concentrating.
- Slower reactions.
- Poorer memory.
- Difficulty making decisions.
- More mistakes.
- Reduced emotional control.

The danger is that sleep-deprived people do not always recognise how impaired they are.

You may feel you are still "fine", while your reaction time and judgement have declined significantly.

## Sleep loss affects the whole body

### Cardiovascular system

Long-term insufficient sleep is associated with a higher risk of:

- High blood pressure.
- Cardiovascular disease.
- Stroke.

During normal sleep, blood pressure and cardiovascular activity change according to the [[nhip-sinh-hoc|circadian rhythm]]. Sleep loss or frequently interrupted sleep can adversely affect this regulation.

### Immune system

Sleep and the immune system are closely linked.

Sleep loss can change:

- The activity of immune cells.
- Inflammatory responses.
- The ability to fight off some pathogens.

So getting enough sleep is an important part of keeping the immune system working normally.

### Metabolism and hunger

Sleep loss can also affect:

- Glucose regulation.
- Insulin sensitivity.
- The hormones involved in hunger and fullness.
- The way we choose food.

After a night of poor sleep, many people tend to want more energy-dense foods. If this continues, sleep loss can contribute to a higher risk of weight gain and metabolic disorders.

## Microsleep: when the brain falls asleep even though you want to stay awake

One of the most dangerous consequences of severe sleep deprivation is **microsleep**, extremely short, involuntary episodes of sleep.

They may last only a few seconds.

During that time:

- The ability to process information drops sharply.
- The person may not respond to their surroundings.
- The sleepy person sometimes does not even realise a microsleep has just happened.

This is especially dangerous when:

- Driving.
- Operating machinery.
- Working at height.
- Doing work that requires quick reactions.

Just a few seconds of lost awareness while moving at high speed can lead to a serious accident.

## Can you catch up on sleep at the weekend?

Sleeping extra after a period of sleep loss can help reduce sleepiness and restore some function.

But the body does not work like a bank account:

**10 hours short during the week → 10 extra hours on Sunday → completely back to normal**

Repeatedly sleeping too little during the week and then sleeping a great deal at the weekend can also make your sleep schedule erratic and affect your circadian rhythm.

The better approach is still to keep your sleep duration and bedtime relatively stable.

## How much sleep do adults need?

Sleep needs vary with age and from person to person.

For most adults, the common target is:

**At least about 7 hours a night**

But the number of hours is not the only factor.

Good sleep also needs to:

- Be rarely interrupted.
- Fit your circadian rhythm.
- Leave your body feeling restored when you wake.

If you sleep long enough but are still severely sleepy during the day, the cause may be related to sleep quality or a disorder such as sleep apnoea.

## Why do some people have insomnia?

One very frustrating paradox of insomnia is:

**The body is exhausted, but when you lie down, the brain refuses to sleep.**

People with insomnia may:

- Have trouble falling asleep.
- Wake up many times during the night.
- Wake too early and be unable to get back to sleep.
- Have poor-quality sleep despite having enough opportunity to sleep.

Short-term insomnia often appears after stress or a change in daily routine.

Chronic insomnia can last for months and significantly affect daytime functioning.

### The brain is too alert just when it needs to sleep

One mechanism studied in insomnia is **hyperarousal**, which can be understood as a state of excessive activation.

You lie still in bed, but the brain is still:

- Thinking about work.
- Going over the events of the day.
- Anticipating what will happen tomorrow.
- Worrying about not being able to sleep.

So the nervous system finds it hard to switch fully from a vigilant state to a resting state.

A person can be **very tired yet still unable to fall asleep easily**.

### The harder you try to sleep, the harder it gets

Insomnia sometimes forms a vicious circle:

**Trouble sleeping → worry about being tired tomorrow → trying to force sleep → checking the clock → more tension → even harder to sleep**

If this loop goes on, the bed and bedtime themselves can become signals that put the brain on alert.

The person starts to think:

**"Will I be able to sleep tonight?"**

And that very worry makes sleeping even harder.


## Conclusion

😴 Sleep is not a time when the body stops working. It is a biological state essential for the brain, immune system, cardiovascular system and metabolism to function normally.

Long-term sleep loss is associated with impaired concentration, poorer memory and many health problems. Insomnia reveals another side: sometimes people **badly want to sleep, but the nervous system remains stuck in a state of vigilance**.

So **getting enough sleep is not time taken away from work**.

It is the time the body and brain need so that the next day we can think, learn and work to the best of our ability.

## How does the Pacific Ocean normally behave?

Under normal conditions, the **trade winds** blow from east to west along the equatorial Pacific.

These winds:

- Push warm water towards Indonesia and Australia.
- Make sea level in the western Pacific higher and the water warmer.
- Allow cold, nutrient-rich water from the deep ocean to well up near the coasts of Peru and Ecuador.

As a result, the western Pacific is usually warm and rainy, while the eastern waters are cooler.

## What happens when El Niño appears?

During an [[el-nino|El Niño]] event, the trade winds **weaken considerably**, and sometimes there are bursts of wind blowing in the opposite direction from usual.

Then:

1. Warm water is no longer pushed strongly westward.
2. Warm water spreads back to the central and eastern Pacific.
3. The upwelling of cold water off South America weakens.
4. Sea-surface temperatures in the central and eastern equatorial Pacific rise.
5. Tropical rain belts and atmospheric circulation shift position.

So El Niño is not simply "warmer seawater".

**The ocean changes → the atmosphere responds → winds and rainfall change → the effects spread to many parts of the world**

This is why a patch of warm water in the middle of the Pacific can affect weather thousands of kilometres away.

## Why can El Niño cause drought in one place but heavy rain in another?

Tropical rainfall is closely linked to areas of warm seawater, where hot, moist air readily rises to form clouds.

When the warm water shifts during El Niño, the areas of heavy rainfall can shift with it.

So some regions may:

- Get unusually heavy rain.
- Face a higher risk of floods and landslides.

While other places may:

- Get too little rain.
- Suffer drought.
- Experience heatwaves.
- Face a higher risk of wildfires.

El Niño is therefore considered one of the phenomena with the strongest influence on year-to-year climate on a global scale.

## How does El Niño affect South American fisheries?

Off Peru and Ecuador, cold water from the deep ocean usually brings plenty of nutrients up near the surface.

These nutrients feed:

**Plankton → small fish → larger fish → seabirds and the wider ecosystem**

When El Niño weakens the upwelling of cold water, the supply of nutrients to the surface layer can fall.

As a result, biological productivity and the distribution of many fish species can change sharply, affecting the ecosystem and the local fishing industry.

Not all the fish necessarily "die off en masse", but the food chain and fishing grounds can be significantly disrupted.

## How does El Niño affect Vietnam?

Vietnam lies on the western side of the Pacific, so it is significantly affected by ENSO.

However:

**El Niño does not produce the same weather pattern every year.**

The impacts also depend on:

- The strength of the El Niño.
- When it appears.
- The monsoon.
- The temperature of the surrounding seas.
- Other climate oscillations.

Even so, during many El Niño events Vietnam faces a higher risk of some of the following.

### Heat

Average temperatures tend to be higher, and the risk of severe heatwaves may increase.

### Drought and water shortages

Some regions may receive less rainfall than usual, especially in seasons and areas sensitive to ENSO.

This increases the risk of:

- Drought.
- Shortages of domestic water.
- Shortages of irrigation water.
- Lower water levels in reservoirs.

### Saltwater intrusion in the Mekong Delta

If rainfall and the flow of the Mekong River fall during the dry season, seawater can push further into the river mouths.

This affects:

- Fresh water.
- Rice production.
- Fruit trees.
- Aquaculture.

However, the extent of saltwater intrusion depends not only on El Niño but also on upstream flow, tides, rainfall and water management.

## Does El Niño make Earth hotter?

It can, but it must be distinguished from climate change.

El Niño releases and redistributes large amounts of heat between the ocean and the atmosphere, so strong El Niño years tend to make **global average temperature temporarily higher**.

But:

**El Niño ≠ climate change**

El Niño is a natural oscillation that plays out over a few years.

Human-caused climate change is a long-term warming trend lasting many decades.

You can picture it this way:

**Climate change raises the baseline temperature**

while:

**El Niño temporarily pushes temperatures higher on top of that baseline**

The two can occur at the same time and make some climate extremes more pronounced.

## How does El Niño end?

El Niño is part of the **ENSO** system.

ENSO is usually divided into three states:

| State | Equatorial Pacific |
|---|---|
| **El Niño** | Warmer than normal |
| **Neutral** | Close to normal |
| **La Niña** | Cooler than normal |

After an El Niño, the system can return to the neutral state or switch to La Niña.

La Niña is not simply "El Niño in reverse", but many of its ocean and atmospheric features tend to be the opposite.

## Conclusion

🌊 El Niño begins with a seemingly small change in temperature over the vast waters of the Pacific, but it can alter the atmosphere's entire system of winds and rainfall.

The chain reaction can be pictured like this:

**Trade winds weaken → warm water shifts east → cold upwelling weakens → rain belts shift → weather changes in many parts of the world**

For Vietnam, El Niño usually means keeping a particularly close watch on **heat, low rainfall, drought, water resources and saltwater intrusion**.

But El Niño is not a "weather switch" with a fixed outcome. Every event is different, and the final impact also depends on many factors in the climate system.

This very connection between ocean and atmosphere reveals something remarkable: **a patch of warming sea in the middle of the Pacific can help change the weather in a place thousands of kilometres away.**

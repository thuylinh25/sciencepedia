## Cardiac arrest does not mean every cell has died

When the heart stops pumping blood, circulation to the brain and organs is interrupted.

After only a short time, the person may:

- Lose consciousness.
- Stop breathing normally.
- Have no effective circulation.

However, this is exactly the stage at which **CPR, defibrillation and other resuscitation measures** can restore circulation in some cases.

So a cardiac arrest that may still be reversible by resuscitation must be distinguished from **death having been determined**.

## The brain is especially sensitive to lack of oxygen

The brain needs a continuous supply of oxygen and glucose.

When circulation stops, neurons quickly lose the ability to function normally, and ischaemic damage begins to develop.

There is no precise clock such as:

**4 minutes = still saveable**

**6 minutes = brain completely dead**

The extent of the damage depends on many factors, such as:

- How long circulation stopped for.
- Whether CPR was performed.
- Body temperature.
- The cause of the cardiac arrest.
- The person's prior state of health.

In some special cases, particularly when the body has been cooled, the chances of recovery can be significantly different.

## Brain death is different from coma

**Brain death** is not the same as coma or a vegetative state.

A person in a coma still has brain activity at varying levels.

Brain death by neurological criteria is the permanent loss of the brain functions required under the applicable medical standards, including the loss of the capacity for consciousness and of essential brainstem functions.

It is a condition from which recovery is impossible.

## What happens to the body after death?

Once circulation and vital functions have permanently ceased, the body begins to undergo a series of post-mortem changes.

### The body begins to cool

The body no longer maintains its temperature through metabolism and circulation.

Over time, body temperature gradually approaches the temperature of the surroundings.

This phenomenon is called:

**Algor mortis**

The rate of cooling depends on the environment, clothing, body size and many other factors.

### Blood settles under gravity

When the heart stops pumping, blood is no longer circulated.

Under gravity, blood collects in the lowest parts of the body, creating areas of reddish-purple discolouration on the skin.

This phenomenon is called:

**Livor mortis**

### The body stiffens

After death, cells no longer maintain normal ATP production.

ATP is needed for the **actin and myosin** filaments in muscle to separate after contracting.

As ATP runs out, these bonds stay locked and the muscles become stiff.

This is:

**Rigor mortis**

Rigor mortis usually appears in the first hours after death, develops gradually and then disappears as tissue decomposition progresses.

The timings are not entirely fixed, because they are strongly influenced by temperature and the condition of the body.

## Cells begin to break themselves down

When oxygen is no longer supplied, cells lose the ability to maintain their chemical balance.

Membranes and cell structures gradually weaken, and [[enzyme|enzymes]] inside are released and begin breaking down the body's own tissues.

This process is called:

**Autolysis – self-digestion**

At the same time, microbes, especially the bacteria that normally live in the digestive tract, take part in post-mortem decomposition.

How quickly this happens depends very strongly on:

- Temperature.
- Humidity.
- The environment.
- The condition of the body.
- Access for microbes and insects.

## What is the brain doing in the final moments?

This is one of the most intriguing areas, but also one of the most easily exaggerated.

Some EEG studies in humans and animals have recorded changes in the brain's electrical activity around the time circulation stops, including changes in the gamma band in some cases.

Gamma activity usually appears in many neural processes related to information processing and cognition.

But this **does not prove that a dying person is clearly conscious**.

In particular, we cannot yet conclude that gamma activity is the cause of experiences such as:

- Seeing a light.
- Feeling of leaving the body.
- Feeling of peace.
- "Life flashing before your eyes."

EEG data from humans at this moment are still very limited, and interpreting the signals is extremely difficult.

## Are near-death experiences real?

**Near-death experiences (NDEs)** are real subjective experiences in the sense that many people who survived critical conditions have reported them.

Descriptions may include:

- Bright light.
- A sense that time has changed.
- A feeling of separating from the body.
- Vivid memories.
- A feeling of peace.

But science has not yet identified a single mechanism that produces NDEs.

Many hypotheses are being studied, involving:

- Lack of oxygen.
- Changes in carbon dioxide.
- Abnormal activity in neural networks.
- [[chat-dan-truyen-than-kinh|Neurotransmitters]].
- Memory.
- Extreme physiological changes when the body is under threat.

So NDEs remain an open scientific topic.

## Where do the body's atoms go?

When a person dies, **the organised biological structure of the body gradually breaks down**, but the atoms that make up the body do not simply disappear.

Carbon, oxygen, hydrogen, nitrogen, calcium and many other elements can return to the environment through physical, chemical and biological processes.

They can:

- Enter the soil.
- Dissolve into water.
- Enter the atmosphere.
- Take part in biogeochemical cycles.
- Become part of other organisms.

This does not mean each atom will "last forever" in exactly its present form, but simply that matter continues to take part in nature's processes of transformation.

## And what about consciousness?

This is where science does not have a final answer to every philosophical question.

Neuroscience shows that consciousness is closely tied to the organised activity of the brain.

When brain function is permanently lost, we have no scientific evidence confirming that individual consciousness continues to exist independently of the brain.

But explaining **what consciousness really is, and how the physical activity of the brain gives rise to subjective experience**, remains one of the great problems of neuroscience and the philosophy of mind.

## Conclusion

🧠 Death is not a moment when all the trillions of cells "switch off" at once.

It is a **biological process**, beginning with the loss of essential functions and continuing with chemical, cellular and tissue changes.

The heart can stop before every cell has died. Different tissues lose their viability at different rates. Afterwards, biological structures gradually break down and matter returns to Earth's natural cycles.

As for the deepest question:

**"What does consciousness experience at the final boundary?"**

it still has no complete answer.

And it is that very gap that makes death not only a subject for medicine, but also one of the deepest questions about the nature of life and consciousness.

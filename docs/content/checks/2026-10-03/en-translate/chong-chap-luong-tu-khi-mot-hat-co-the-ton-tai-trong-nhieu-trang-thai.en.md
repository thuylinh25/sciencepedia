## What is quantum superposition?

In classical physics, an object is usually described by definite quantities such as position or velocity.

In quantum mechanics, the state of a system is described by a **wave function**. If a system can exist in state A and state B, quantum mechanics allows its general state to be a combination of both. 

A simple way to picture it:

**Classical world: A or B**

**Quantum world: a superposition of A and B**

This does not simply mean that the particle has secretly "chosen" A or B and we just don't know which yet. The components of a superposition can **interfere quantum-mechanically** with one another, producing results that no classical probabilistic mixture can explain.

## Schrödinger's cat

In 1935, the physicist Erwin Schrödinger proposed a famous thought experiment to show how strange things become when quantum mechanics is applied to large objects.

Imagine a cat inside a sealed box together with:

- A radioactive atom.
- A Geiger counter.
- A mechanism that releases poison.

If the atom decays, the mechanism is triggered and the cat dies. If the atom has not decayed, the cat stays alive.

If the whole system is described purely by quantum evolution, the state of the atom becomes entangled with the state of the cat, giving the famous image:

**"The cat is both alive and dead."**

This is not a claim that real cats in everyday life regularly exist that way. Schrödinger offered the example to highlight the **measurement problem** and the strange difference between the quantum world and the macroscopic world. 

## Why don't we see objects "in two places at once"?

One important reason is **decoherence**.

A well-isolated particle can maintain a superposition.

But a large object constantly interacts with:

- Light [[photon|photons]].
- Air molecules.
- Thermal radiation.
- Surrounding objects.

These interactions cause quantum information to leak quickly into the environment, so the ability to observe interference between the components of the superposition vanishes extremely fast.

This is why the macroscopic world around us appears to obey classical physics. Experiments at NIST have also directly studied the decoherence of superpositions created in trapped atoms. 

## What happens when you measure a quantum system?

When you measure a system that is in a superposition, you get **one definite result**.

For example, a qubit can be prepared in a superposition of:

**0 and 1**

but when it is measured, the final result will be:

**0 or 1**

with probabilities determined by the quantum state before the measurement. 

What really happens to the wave function during this process is still tied to one of the foundational questions of quantum mechanics, and there are several different interpretations.

## How does superposition make quantum computers possible?

Ordinary computers use **bits**:

**0 or 1**

Quantum computers use **qubits**, which can exist in a superposition of:

**0 and 1**

When many qubits are combined, the system can describe a state space that grows very rapidly with the number of qubits.

However, this **does not mean that a quantum computer simply tries all the answers at once and reads them out**.

Quantum algorithms have to control:

- Superposition.
- Quantum interference.
- Quantum entanglement.

in order to amplify the probability of the desired result and suppress the unwanted ones.

It is this mechanism that gives quantum computers the potential to excel at **certain kinds of problems**, not at every computation. 

## Is quantum cryptography related?

Yes, but it should be distinguished from quantum computing.

Some **quantum key distribution (QKD)** schemes use the quantum states of photons to detect eavesdropping.

If a third party measures the quantum states in an unsuitable way, the measurement can change the statistics of the signal. The two communicating parties can then detect an abnormal level of noise and discard the insecure key.

The key point is not simply that "the hacker collapses the wave function", but that the laws of quantum mechanics mean that measuring or copying an arbitrary quantum state leaves detectable traces.

## Superposition doesn't just exist on paper

Superpositions have been created in many physical systems.

A famous experiment at NIST once put a beryllium ion into a quantum state made of two motional states separated in space by more than **80 nanometres**, about 11 times larger than the original size of the ion's state. 

Experiments like this show that superposition is not a philosophical metaphor.

It is a real physical phenomenon that can be:

- Prepared.
- Controlled.
- Made to interfere.
- And measured.

## Conclusion

⚛️ Quantum superposition is one of the strangest features of nature: the state of a quantum system can be a combination of several different possibilities, and those possibilities can interfere with one another.

In the microscopic world, this phenomenon is observed directly. But in the macroscopic world, constant interaction with the environment causes **decoherence**, so fragile superpositions quickly lose their ability to interfere. 

This seemingly absurd principle has now become the foundation of a new generation of technologies, from quantum computers to quantum communication.

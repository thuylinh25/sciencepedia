## Caffeine does not create energy

While we are awake, a substance called **adenosine** gradually builds up and acts on receptors in the brain, helping to increase the feeling of sleepiness.

Caffeine is similar enough in structure to adenosine to occupy these receptors, but it does not activate them in the same way.

The result:

**Adenosine is still there → caffeine blocks the receptors → the feeling of sleepiness temporarily decreases**

In other words, caffeine does not really give the body extra energy.

It mainly helps you **feel less tired for a while**.

## Why does caffeine make us alert?

When the action of adenosine is limited, the activity of many neural networks changes.

Caffeine can help:

- Increase alertness.
- Reduce the feeling of fatigue.
- Improve attention in the short term.
- Speed up reaction times on some tasks.

Caffeine also indirectly affects neurotransmitter systems such as [[dopamine]] and norepinephrine.

That is why after a cup of coffee we often feel more alert and ready to work.

## How are coffee and tea different?

Both coffee and tea contain caffeine, but the amount depends heavily on the raw material and how it is brewed.

An ordinary cup of coffee usually contains more caffeine than a cup of tea.

So coffee usually feels more noticeably stimulating.

### Tea's "secret weapon": L-theanine

Tea also contains **L-theanine**, a natural [[axit-amin|amino acid]] that can affect the activity of the nervous system.

The combination of caffeine and L-theanine has been studied for its potential to support:

- Alertness.
- Attention.
- Concentration.

This may help explain why some people describe tea as producing a feeling of being **alert but gentler** than coffee.

However, this should not be taken to mean that L-theanine makes the caffeine in tea "slow-release". Caffeine is still absorbed and metabolised by the body through its own mechanisms.

## Why does drinking too much make you jittery?

Caffeine does not only act on sleepiness.

At high enough doses, sensitive people may experience:

- Restlessness.
- Shaky hands.
- A racing heart.
- Anxiety.
- Difficulty sleeping.
- Digestive discomfort.

How strongly people react depends a great deal on:

- Genetics.
- Body weight.
- Medications being taken.
- Caffeine habits.
- State of health.

The FDA says that for **most healthy adults**, a total of about **400 mg of caffeine a day** is not generally associated with adverse effects, but this is not a suitable threshold for everyone. 

## Why does coffee seem to "weaken" over time?

With regular caffeine use, the body can develop **tolerance** to some of its effects.

As a result, an amount of caffeine that once made you very alert may become less noticeable.

If a regular drinker suddenly stops caffeine, withdrawal symptoms may appear, such as:

- Headache.
- Sleepiness.
- Fatigue.
- Difficulty concentrating.
- Irritability.

This does not mean caffeine supplies energy the body is short of. The body has simply adapted to caffeine regularly acting on the adenosine system.

## Caffeine can disrupt sleep even if you can still fall asleep

Another issue is that caffeine stays in the body for quite a long time.

Even after the feeling of being "wide awake" has gone, some caffeine may still be in the body and affect sleep.

Because the rate of metabolism varies considerably from person to person, there is no single cut-off such as **2 p.m.** that suits everyone.

A more useful rule is:

**If caffeine makes it hard for you to sleep, move your last drink earlier in the day.**

Enough good-quality sleep is usually more important than trying to use more caffeine to fight the effects of sleep deprivation.

## Are coffee and tea good for the brain in the long term?

Coffee and tea contain many bioactive compounds besides caffeine, including polyphenols.

Many observational studies have found associations between drinking coffee or tea and some positive health outcomes.

But it is important to distinguish:

**Association ≠ proven cause**

It should not be claimed that drinking 2–3 cups a day will definitely prevent Alzheimer's or Parkinson's.

Brain health also depends on many factors, such as:

- Genetics.
- Physical activity.
- Sleep.
- Blood pressure and cardiovascular health.
- Diet.
- Smoking and alcohol.
- Age.

## Is coffee or tea better?

There is no single answer for everyone.

### Coffee suits you if you:

- Want a more noticeable dose of caffeine.
- Need a short-term boost in alertness.
- Tolerate caffeine well.

### Tea suits you if you:

- Want a usually lower dose of caffeine.
- Are sensitive to coffee.
- Like the natural combination of caffeine and L-theanine.

More important than choosing tea or coffee is **your total caffeine intake and how your body responds to it**.

## Conclusion

☕ Coffee and tea do not really "pump energy" into the brain.

Caffeine mainly **temporarily blocks adenosine signalling**, so the feeling of tiredness and sleepiness decreases. Tea also contains L-theanine, while coffee usually supplies more caffeine.

So rather than thinking of caffeine as fuel for the brain, a more accurate understanding is:

**Caffeine does not remove the need for rest. It only temporarily makes the brain less able to sense the signal of tiredness.**

And when that effect wears off, what the body still needs most is usually not another cup of coffee, but enough sleep.

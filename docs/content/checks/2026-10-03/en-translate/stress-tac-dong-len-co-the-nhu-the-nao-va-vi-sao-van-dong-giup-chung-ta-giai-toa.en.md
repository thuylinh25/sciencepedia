## The brain switches on "fight or flight" mode

When you worry about work, money or a dangerous situation, the brain starts up a **biological stress response**, and the sympathetic nervous system is activated.

Adrenaline and nerve signals quickly make the body:

- Speed up the heart.
- Change [[huyet-ap|blood pressure]].
- Breathe faster.
- Dilate the pupils.
- Send blood preferentially to the muscles.
- Mobilise glucose and other fuel sources.

Another system, called the **hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis**, is also activated and helps release **[[cortisol]]**.

Cortisol helps the body maintain its energy supply and adapt to a stressful situation that lasts longer.

This is a very useful survival mechanism if you really do have to run away from danger.

But in modern life, the "threat" is sometimes just an email, a meeting or a deadline.

The body still prepares for action even though you are only sitting at a computer.

## Why does long-term stress leave us exhausted?

Acute stress can be useful.

But when the stress system is activated too often or for too long, the body has to stay on alert for long periods.

This can affect:

- Sleep.
- Mood.
- Concentration.
- Blood pressure.
- Metabolism.
- The immune system.
- Pain and muscle tension.

That is why a person can feel:

**Very tired yet still tense and unable to sleep.**

Not because the body is "full of unreleased energy", but because the nervous system and hormones cannot easily return to their normal resting state.

## Why does stress make the body ache?

When stressed, many people unconsciously:

- Clench their jaw.
- Hunch their shoulders.
- Tense their neck muscles.
- Tighten their back muscles.
- Hold their body in a stiff posture for long periods.

If this goes on, the muscles can become sore and tired.

This is **genuine mechanical muscle tension**, and it is a consequence of the nervous system's response to stress.

## Why does exercise help reduce stress?

Exercise is not simply "burning off accumulated stress".

It acts on many of the body's systems at the same time.

### Moving the body from preparation to action

The stress response evolved to prepare the body for physical action.

When you run, walk briskly, cycle or do strength training, the body actually uses:

- Glucose.
- Fat.
- Oxygen.
- ATP.

for muscle activity.

After the workout, the nervous system can gradually shift from an activated state to recovery.

### Changing brain activity

Exercise affects many neural systems involved in:

- Mood.
- Motivation.
- Pain.
- Stress.

Molecules such as [[he-endocannabinoid|endocannabinoids]], endorphins and many other neuromodulators may contribute to the pleasant feeling after exercise.

### Helping the muscles relax

During exercise, muscles go through cycles of:

**Contract → relax → contract → relax**

After a suitable workout, muscle tension from sitting too long or from stress may decrease.

### Improving sleep

Regular exercise can also improve sleep quality.

Good sleep in turn helps the nervous system regulate stress more effectively the next day.

This creates a positive feedback loop:

**Exercise → better sleep → better stress regulation → better recovery**


## Short-term and chronic stress are very different

Stress is not always bad.

### Short-term stress

Can help:

- Increase alertness.
- Sharpen reactions.
- Mobilise energy.
- Prepare the body for action.

### Long-term stress

Without time to recover, chronic stress can contribute to:

- Insomnia.
- Anxiety.
- Exhaustion.
- Muscle pain.
- Difficulty concentrating.
- Adverse cardiovascular and metabolic changes.

So the goal is not to **eliminate all stress**, but to help the body be able to return to balance after the stressful situation is over.

## Conclusion

🧠 Stress is not a kind of "psychological energy" trapped in the body, and exercise is not simply burning off that energy.

Stress is **a coordinated response of the brain, nervous system, hormones and metabolism** that prepares the body to cope with a challenge.

Exercise helps reduce stress because it acts on these very systems: using energy for the muscles, changing nerve signals, supporting relaxation, improving sleep and increasing the capacity to recover.

A simple way to picture it:

**Stress → the body switches into a state of readiness for action**

**Exercise → the body carries out the activity and then switches to recovery**

So when you feel stressed after hours in front of a screen, getting up for a walk or a workout is not a way of "discharging stress energy" in a physical sense.

**It is a way of helping the nervous system and the body move from a prolonged state of alert back to a state of recovery.**

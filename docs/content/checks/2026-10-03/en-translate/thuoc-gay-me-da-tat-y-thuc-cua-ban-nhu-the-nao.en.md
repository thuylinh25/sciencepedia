## Anaesthesia is not the same as sleep

During natural sleep, the brain keeps working through different cycles, and you can wake up if a stimulus is strong enough.

In general anaesthesia, drugs produce a different state:

- Loss of consciousness.
- No memories formed of the operation.
- Pain reduced or controlled.
- No movement in response to surgical stimulation.

Importantly, this state is **reversible**. When the drugs are reduced or stopped, activity in the neural networks gradually returns and the patient wakes up.

## What does an anaesthetic need to achieve?

Anaesthetists usually combine several drugs to control different components.

### Unconsciousness

The patient is not aware of what is happening during the operation.

### Pain relief

Painkillers help limit pain signals and the body's response to surgical stimulation.

### Amnesia

Some drugs reduce the ability to form new memories, so patients usually do not remember the operation.

### Immobility

In some operations, muscle relaxants are used to keep the body still and make surgery easier.

Not every anaesthetic produces all of these effects. That is why doctors usually use **a combination of several drugs** rather than relying on a single one.

## How do anaesthetics act on the brain?

The brain works through billions of neurons constantly passing signals to one another.

Anaesthetic drugs change the activity of many types of receptors and ion channels on these neurons.

### Boosting inhibitory signals

Some anaesthetics, such as propofol, increase the activity of the **GABA** system, one of the brain's most important inhibitory neurotransmitter systems.

You can think of GABA as a "brake" that reduces neurons' ability to fire signals.

When this effect is strengthened, activity in many neural networks falls.

### Reducing excitatory signals

Some other drugs, most notably ketamine, act on the **NMDA** receptor for glutamate.

Glutamate is one of the brain's most important excitatory [[chat-dan-truyen-than-kinh|neurotransmitters]].

When this pathway is altered, the ability of neurons to exchange and integrate information changes too.

## Where does consciousness go?

There is no single "consciousness switch" in the brain.

Instead, consciousness depends on the ability of many brain regions to exchange information with one another.

Anaesthetics change the activity of, and the connections between:

- The cerebral cortex.
- The thalamus.
- The networks that maintain wakefulness.
- The regions that process sensation and memory.

When these networks no longer coordinate normally, the brain cannot integrate information into a complete conscious experience.

Put simply:

**The brain is still active, but the networks that create wakefulness no longer communicate in the normal way.**

## Why don't you feel time passing?

Under anaesthesia, the ability to form new memories is severely impaired.

You may remember:

**"I was lying in the operating theatre."**

And then the next memory is:

**"I woke up in the recovery room."**

There is no chain of memories in between for the brain to use as time markers.

So an operation lasting several hours can be experienced subjectively as just a moment.

## What does an anaesthetist actually do?

Throughout the operation, the anaesthetist does not simply "give the drugs and wait for the patient to wake up".

They continuously monitor and adjust:

- Heart rate.
- Blood pressure.
- Blood oxygen levels.
- Breathing.
- Carbon dioxide levels.
- Body temperature.
- The level of anaesthetic and the patient's response.

Some anaesthetics and muscle relaxants can also reduce the ability to breathe unaided, so patients may need breathing support during surgery.

When the operation is over, the drugs are reduced or stopped. Some drugs have antagonists that reverse their effects, while many others are simply metabolised or eliminated by the body.

Activity in the neural networks gradually recovers and consciousness returns.

## The strangest thing about anaesthesia

After more than 150 years of using anaesthetics, science understands a good deal about the molecules and neural networks the drugs act on, but there is still no single mechanism that explains the whole anaesthetic state.

Different drugs act on different molecular targets, and loss of consciousness, amnesia, pain relief and immobility do not necessarily arise from the same part of the nervous system.

That is why anaesthesia research also helps scientists approach a bigger question:

**How is human consciousness produced?**

🧠 Anaesthesia does not really "switch off" the brain. It temporarily changes the way billions of neurons coordinate and exchange information. When the network that creates consciousness is disrupted, the world disappears from your experience. When the drugs wear off, the network reconnects and you wake up, usually with no memory of the time in between.

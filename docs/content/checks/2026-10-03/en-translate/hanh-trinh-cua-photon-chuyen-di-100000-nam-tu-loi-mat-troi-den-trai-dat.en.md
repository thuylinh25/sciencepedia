## Energy is produced in the Sun's core

The journey begins in the **Sun's core**, where the temperature is about 15 million K.

There, [[phan-ung-nhiet-hach|nuclear fusion]] reactions turn hydrogen into helium and release energy. Part of this energy appears as high-energy [[buc-xa-dien-tu|electromagnetic radiation]], together with neutrinos and the thermal motion of the plasma.

But the radiation cannot fly straight out of the Sun.

## A "random walk" through the radiative zone

The inside of the Sun is extremely dense, hot plasma.

[[photon|Photons]] travel only a very short distance before interacting with matter. The energy keeps going through the processes of:

- Absorption.
- Re-emission.
- Scattering.
- Changes of direction.

Because the direction of travel keeps changing, the energy follows a path like a drunkard's stagger, called a **random walk**.

If light could fly straight from the centre to the surface at the speed of light, the trip would take just over 2 seconds. But because of this diffusion, the energy can take about **tens of thousands to hundreds of thousands of years** to reach the outer layers. NASA's Sun fact page gives a figure of about **170,000 years**, while another NASA document says that most calculations give results between 10,000 and 170,000 years. 

## It is not the same photon all the way

This is the point most easily misunderstood.

You should not imagine a gamma-ray photon being born in the core, colliding trillions of times and finally turning into a [[anh-sang-kha-kien|visible-light]] photon that flies to Earth.

Inside the Sun, photons are constantly absorbed and new photons are emitted.

So:

**What takes tens of thousands to hundreds of thousands of years to escape is the energy, not a single photon.** 

Along the way, the energy is redistributed between the plasma and the radiation field. By the time it reaches the outer layers, the spectrum of the radiation is very different from the high-energy radiation in the core.

## The convective zone: energy carried up by matter

On reaching the **convective zone**, radiation is no longer the most efficient way to transport energy.

Hot plasma from below rises, carrying energy towards the surface. After cooling, the material sinks again.

This process is like water boiling in a gigantic pot, and it creates the granular structures seen on the surface of the Sun.

## The photon is finally free

When the energy reaches the **photosphere**, the density of matter has fallen enough for photons to escape the Sun without quickly being reabsorbed.

These are the photons of light that we can receive on Earth.

From here on, the journey becomes very fast.

The average distance between Earth and the Sun is about:

**150 million km**

At a speed of nearly:

**300,000 km/s**

a photon needs only about:

**8 minutes 20 seconds**

to reach Earth.

## When the photon reaches Earth

After its journey through space, a photon may:

- Be absorbed or scattered by the atmosphere.
- Warm the land and the oceans.
- Be absorbed by plants for photosynthesis.
- Transfer its energy to a solar cell.
- Reflect off an object and enter your eye.

If the photon enters your eye, the [[vong-mac|retina]] absorbs it and turns the light information into nerve signals from which the brain builds an image.

## Two completely different timescales

The journey of the Sun's energy can be summed up like this:

**Sun's core → tens of thousands to hundreds of thousands of years → Sun's surface → 8 minutes 20 seconds → Earth**

☀️ So the sunlight touching your skin is both young and ancient: **the particular photon left the Sun's surface just over 8 minutes ago, but the energy it carries originated in fusion reactions deep inside the Sun long before that.**

In the familiar world, an object has a fairly definite position, cannot spontaneously pass through a wall, and two objects far apart cannot share strange instantaneous correlations.

But when we go down to the atomic and subatomic scale, that intuition starts to stop working. Electrons, [[photon|photons]] and quantum systems follow rules very different from those of objects in everyday life.

This is the world of **quantum mechanics**.

## What is quantum mechanics?

Quantum mechanics is the physical framework that describes matter and radiation at very small scales.

It is used to study:

- Atoms.
- Electrons.
- Photons.
- Molecules.
- Subatomic particles.
- Many other quantum systems of matter.

Interestingly, the classical laws are not really "wrong". They still describe the macroscopic world very well.

Classical mechanics can be seen as an approximate limit that appears when quantum effects become negligible in large systems.

## Wave–particle duality

In classical physics, waves and particles are two very different concepts.

But in the quantum world, this division is no longer simple.

Light can show:

- Interference.
- Diffraction.
- Polarisation.

which are characteristic of waves.

But light also exchanges energy in quanta called **photons**.

Conversely, electrons, usually pictured as particles, can also produce interference patterns.

That does not mean an electron or a photon sometimes turns into a wave and sometimes into a marble.

**"Wave" and "particle" are classical concepts used to describe different aspects of a quantum entity.**

## The Heisenberg uncertainty principle

One of the most famous principles of quantum mechanics is the **Heisenberg uncertainty principle**.

It says that we cannot have a state with both **position** and **momentum** arbitrarily precise at the same time.

Put simply:

**The more precisely position is defined → the more uncertain momentum is**

**The more precisely momentum is defined → the more uncertain position is**

This is not simply because microscopes or measuring instruments are not yet good enough.

The uncertainty is built into the mathematical structure of quantum states.

## Quantum superposition

A quantum system can exist in a **superposition of several states**.

For example, a qubit can be prepared in a superposition of:

**0 and 1**

Before measurement, it should not be pictured simply as a classical bit that has secretly chosen 0 or 1 without our knowing.

The components of a superposition can **interfere quantum-mechanically** with one another.

This is one of the fundamental differences between quantum probability and ordinary lack of information.

## Schrödinger's cat

In 1935, Erwin Schrödinger proposed a famous thought experiment.

A cat is placed in a sealed box together with:

- A radioactive atom.
- A radiation detector.
- A mechanism that releases poison.

If the atom decays, the cat dies.

If the atom does not decay, the cat lives.

If the whole system is described by quantum evolution, the state of the atom becomes linked to the state of the cat, leading to the famous image:

**"The cat is both alive and dead."**

Schrödinger did not offer this example to claim that real cats regularly exist that way.

He wanted to point to a deep question:

**Why do superpositions appear clearly in the microscopic world, yet we do not see them in everyday life?**

An important part of the modern answer involves **decoherence**, the loss of quantum coherence when a system interacts with its environment.

## Quantum tunnelling: can a particle "walk through walls"?

In classical mechanics, if a ball does not have enough energy to get over a wall, it bounces back.

In quantum mechanics, things are different.

A particle's wave function can extend into and beyond a **potential barrier**.

So there is a probability of finding the particle on the other side, even when, according to classical physics, it does not have enough energy to cross the barrier.

This phenomenon is called:

**Quantum tunnelling**

It is not just a strange theory.

Quantum tunnelling plays a role in:

- Radioactive decay.
- Nuclear reactions in stars.
- Scanning tunnelling microscopes.
- Some electronic components.

## Quantum entanglement

Perhaps the most astonishing phenomenon is **quantum entanglement**.

Two or more quantum systems can form a joint state in which the state of each part cannot be described completely independently.

When entangled particles are measured, the results can show correlations stronger than some classical physical models allow.

Albert Einstein once called the related phenomenon a kind of **"spooky action at a distance"**.

Later experiments on entanglement confirmed the violation of Bell inequalities and became the basis of the 2022 Nobel Prize in Physics. 

## Does entanglement transmit information faster than light?

No.

This is a very common misconception.

The measurement results of entangled particles show special correlations, but each individual measurement still gives a probabilistic result.

An observer on one side cannot choose their result in order to send an instant message to the other side.

So:

**Quantum entanglement ≠ faster-than-light communication**

It does not let you send an instant email from Earth to a galaxy millions of [[nam-anh-sang|light-years]] away.

## Does quantum mechanics really exist only in the microscopic world?

The quantum laws do not suddenly disappear when objects get bigger.

The problem is that large systems constantly interact with their environment:

- Air.
- Light.
- Heat.
- Surrounding atoms.

These interactions quickly destroy the ability to observe interference between superposed states.

That process is called **decoherence**.

Superpositions and entangled states have been created in a controlled way in atoms, photons and even small mechanical oscillators, showing that the boundary between the "quantum world" and the "classical world" is more complicated than a simple split into small and large.


## Conclusion

⚛️ Quantum mechanics does not really "break all the laws of physics".

It shows that the familiar laws of the macroscopic world are only part of a much bigger picture.

At the quantum level:

- Particles can exist in superpositions.
- Position and momentum are subject to an uncertainty limit.
- Particles can tunnel through potential barriers.
- Systems far apart can carry entangled correlations with no simple equivalent in classical physics.

These phenomena, which sound like science fiction, have now been created, measured and controlled in the laboratory, and they have become the foundation for quantum computing, sensing and networks.

Perhaps the strangest thing about quantum mechanics is not that it is hard to believe.

**The strangest thing is that after more than a century of testing, nature keeps behaving according to those strange quantum rules with astonishing precision.**

## 🌌 The Solar System does more than circle the Milky Way

The Solar System completes one orbit around the centre of the Milky Way in about:

> **200–265 million years**

This figure is derived from the Sun's distance to the Galactic centre and an assumed rotation speed, so it depends on both: with the commonly used rotation speed of 220 km/s, the period is about 235 million years.

But that orbit does not lie entirely in one fixed plane.

Under the gravitational pull of the Galactic disc, the Solar System also oscillates up and down through the plane of the Milky Way, like a buoy bobbing on waves.

According to the model of the disc's gravitational potential built by Bahcall & Bahcall (1985), this oscillation has a period of about:

> **52–74 million years for one complete cycle**

equivalent to about:

> **26–37 million years between two crossings of the Galactic plane** (half the cycle above)

This wide range reflects uncertainty about the amount of dark matter in the disc, as well as about the Solar System's current position and vertical velocity. The period is not fixed either: it changes as the Solar System's distance from the Galactic centre changes.

It is precisely this interval that once caught the attention of many researchers, because it is close to some of the extinction cycles proposed from the fossil record.

---

## ☄️ The Shiva hypothesis: when the Milky Way becomes an agent of destruction

An idea proposed in the 1980s (Rampino & Stothers, 1984) holds that each time the Solar System passes through the matter-dense region of the Galactic disc, Earth may face greater risks than usual.

This hypothesis is sometimes called:

> **The Shiva hypothesis**

— a name coined by Rampino and Haggerty in a 1996 paper.

According to the hypothesis, crossings of the Galactic plane may trigger events that raise the risk of mass extinction on Earth.

It is important to note, however:

> This is still a debated hypothesis and has not been confirmed.

A review by Bailer-Jones (2009) concluded that there is very little evidence for genuine cycles in biodiversity, impact craters or climate on timescales of tens to hundreds of millions of years, and argued that crossing the plane or the spiral arms of the Milky Way probably has little effect on the biosphere and climate above background levels. On the other side, Melott and Bambach (2013) still found a signal of about 27 million years in marine extinction rates after updating the geological timescale — but they themselves wrote that the signal suggests, rather than requires, an astronomical role.

---

## 🌠 The first threat: comet showers from the Oort Cloud

Far beyond the Solar System lies a hypothesised region called:

> **The [[dam-may-oort|Oort Cloud]]**

According to NASA, it may contain hundreds of billions, or even trillions, of icy bodies, with an inner edge thought to lie about 2,000–5,000 times the Earth–Sun distance from the Sun and an outer edge somewhere between 10,000 and 100,000 times that distance.

When the Solar System passes through denser regions of the Milky Way:

- Interstellar gas clouds.
- Stars passing relatively close by.
- The Galactic gravitational field.

can disturb the orbits in the Oort Cloud.

Some comets get "kicked" into the inner Solar System and become objects that could potentially collide with Earth.

Even so, no proposed mechanism explains both the timing and the strength of such a cycle: molecular clouds are spread too widely around the Galactic plane to produce a regular rhythm, and the ordinary matter in the disc is not concentrated closely enough to the plane (Randall & Reece, 2014). To rescue this scenario, Randall and Reece had to additionally assume a thin disc of dark matter — a separate hypothesis that they themselves say still awaits observational testing.

The idea nonetheless remains appealing because:

> A large impact from space really did cause the K-Pg mass extinction about 66 million years ago, the event that ended the age of the dinosaurs.

But the two must be kept apart: the Chicxulub impactor has been identified as a carbonaceous-type asteroid that formed beyond the orbit of Jupiter, not a comet from the Oort Cloud (Fischer-Gödde et al., 2024). So K-Pg shows that an impact can cause a mass extinction, but it is not evidence for a Galaxy-triggered comet-shower scenario.

---

## 🌫️ The second threat: interstellar dust and climate change

Another hypothesis holds that the Solar System may sometimes pass through regions rich in interstellar dust.

If enough dust entered the Solar System or Earth's atmosphere:

- The amount of sunlight reaching the surface could fall.
- The global climate could change.
- Ecosystems could come under heavy pressure.

A scenario of global cooling when the Solar System meets an interstellar cloud has been proposed in the scientific literature, but to date it remains only a theoretically possible mechanism; there is no evidence that it was ever the main cause of any mass extinction (Bailer-Jones, 2009).

---

## 🌟 Another hypothesis: passing through the spiral arms

Another line of thought, proposed by some authors, holds that if the Milky Way does influence life and climate on Earth, the cause may lie in passing through:

> **The Galaxy's spiral arms**

These are regions with concentrations of:

- More gas and dust.
- More young stars.
- More massive stars.

Giant stars are short-lived and often end as:

- [[sieu-tan-tinh|Supernovae]].
- Supernova remnants, which are thought to produce most of the Milky Way's cosmic rays.

So each time the Solar System passes through a spiral arm, the surrounding cosmic environment may become more dangerous.

But there are several reasons for caution. The density of stars inside a spiral arm is only about 1.1 times higher than outside, according to one estimate (other estimates give 1.5–3 times in other galaxies), and massive stars and supernovae also occur outside the arms. The spiral structure of the Milky Way itself is still unclear — two arms or four, and how fast they rotate — so working out when the Solar System passed through the arms in the past is very difficult (Bailer-Jones, 2009). When Overholt, Melott and Pohl (2009) re-examined spiral-arm crossings against new data on the Milky Way's structure, the previously published correlations with climate change on Earth disappeared, and could not be recovered with any reasonable arm rotation speed.

---

## ❓ What does science say today?

Although the idea of a "Galactic extinction cycle" is very appealing, modern research has not reached a consensus.

The main difficulties include:

### The fossil record is imperfect

Pinning down the exact timing of extinctions across hundreds of millions of years of history is not simple.

In the dataset Raup and Sepkoski used in 1984, the average dating error was about 6 million years — nearly a quarter of the 26-million-year cycle they proposed; the ages of impact craters are also often uncertain by about 5 million years (Bailer-Jones, 2009). With errors of that size, a real cycle could be smeared out, while a spurious cycle could emerge from the way geological stages are divided.

### The cycle is not stable

Different studies, of the extinction record or the impact-crater record, have produced:

- 26 million years.
- 30 million years.
- 35 million years.
- Or no clear cycle at all.

Even the roughly 35-million-year cycle in the impact-crater record has only weak statistical support, and it disappears entirely once you account for having searched over a great many possible periods (Randall & Reece, 2014).

### The Solar System's current position

The Solar System currently lies quite close to the Galactic plane: about 15 parsecs to the north according to one estimate cited by Bailer-Jones (2009) (estimates range from 8 to 35 parsecs), while the amplitude of the oscillation is about 70 parsecs. That means the Solar System crossed the plane not long ago.

Yet there has been no major mass extinction in roughly the last 15 million years, and some authors use this very fact as an argument against the hypothesis. One could reply that not every crossing causes a catastrophe; but when you are free to choose which crossings count and which do not, almost any model can be made to fit the geological data, and then there is no longer any cycle left to test.


## 📌 Conclusion

🌌 Earth does not exist in isolation from the rest of the universe. In the roughly 4.6 billion years since the planets formed, the Solar System has passed through very different environments within the Milky Way many times.

Whether crossings of the Galactic plane or of the spiral arms really act as a "reset button" for life on Earth remains an open question. What is clear is that catastrophes can come from beyond Earth — the Chicxulub impact is one example. Whether the Solar System's journey through the Milky Way sets a rhythm for those catastrophes has not been demonstrated; random impacts and mechanisms on Earth itself, such as volcanism, plate tectonics and changes in sea level, remain possible causes of many environmental catastrophes (Bailer-Jones, 2009).

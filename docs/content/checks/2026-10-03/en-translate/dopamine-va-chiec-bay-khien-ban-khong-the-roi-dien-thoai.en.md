## Dopamine is not the "happiness hormone"

Dopamine is a [[chat-dan-truyen-than-kinh|neurotransmitter]] involved in many functions, such as:

- Motivation.
- Learning from rewards.
- Predicting rewards.
- Attention.
- Controlling movement.

Dopamine does not simply create a feeling of pleasure.

One of its important roles is to help the brain learn that:

**"This behaviour is likely to bring something worthwhile — do it again."**

That is why dopamine is closely linked to habit formation.

## Why are TikTok and social media so hard to stop?

Platforms with endlessly scrolling content create a special feature:

**You don't know when the next reward will appear.**

One video may be boring, but the next one is extremely interesting.

Notifications are similar:

- It may be nothing important.
- It may be a message you have been waiting for.
- It may be a like.
- It may be something very appealing.

This uncertainty makes the brain keep wanting to check:

**"What about the next one?"**

Dopamine is involved in predicting and learning from these uncertain rewards.

## Why does reading a book seem more "boring"?

Compare two activities:

### Watching short videos

The reward comes after a few seconds:

Video → interesting → swipe → new video → interesting → swipe again.

### Reading or studying

The reward may take hours, days or even months to arrive:

Study → practise → make mistakes → keep going → improve → reach the goal.

The brain tends to be drawn to rewards that are:

- Faster.
- Easier to get.
- Less effortful.
- Less predictable.

So when your phone is right beside you, reading a chapter of a book can struggle to compete with hundreds of small rewards available after a single swipe of the screen.


## Does a day without TikTok "reset" your dopamine receptors?

There is no good evidence that simply:

- Not using your phone for 24 hours.
- Sitting bored for a day.
- Stopping listening to music.
- Stopping eating tasty food.

will make the dopamine receptors in your brain immediately return to their original state.

The brain has no simple **"Reset dopamine"** button like that.

However, deliberately cutting back on behaviours that feel out of control is still useful, because it:

- Removes the triggers for the habit.
- Creates a gap between urge and action.
- Reduces the number of times you check your phone without thinking.
- Frees up time for more important activities.

The goal is not to reduce dopamine.

The goal is to **change behaviour**.

## More practical ways to control phone scrolling

### Add "friction" to apps

Instead of relying entirely on willpower:

- Turn off unnecessary notifications.
- Move social media off the home screen.
- Log out after each use.
- Keep your phone out of reach while working.
- Use app time limits.

Each small step makes opening the app less automatic.

### Don't start the day with social media

If you open social media as soon as you wake up, it is very easy to switch from:

**"I actively choose what needs doing"**

to:

**"I react to whatever my phone hands me."**

A screen-free period at the start of the day can help you be more deliberate in choosing what to do next.

### Break tasks into small rewards

The brain likes goals where it can see progress.

Instead of:

**"Today I have to study English."**

try:

**"Learn 15 new words."**

Instead of:

**"I have to finish the article."**

start with:

**"Write 200 words."**

Small milestones provide quicker feedback for a task whose reward is otherwise very far away.

### Separate entertainment from work

A simple rule:

**Work first → entertainment afterwards**

For example:

25 minutes of focus → a 5-minute break.

Finish a task → watch a video.

This turns entertainment into a deliberate reward rather than an automatic habit.

## Do you need a full "Dopamine Detox" day?

Not necessarily.

You don't need to:

- Stop listening to music.
- Fast.
- Not talk to other people.
- Give up every enjoyable activity.

These activities also involve dopamine, but dopamine is not the enemy.

If you want to try a low-stimulation day, the goal should simply be to cut back on the behaviours you find hard to control, such as:

- Social media.
- Short videos.
- Games.
- Constantly checking notifications.

Then spend the time on:

- Walking.
- Reading.
- Exercise.
- Meeting people in person.
- Work that needs concentration.
- Getting enough sleep.

## Not all procrastination is down to dopamine

If you regularly can't get started on work, the cause may also involve:

- Lack of sleep.
- Stress.
- Work that is too hard or too vague.
- Anxiety.
- An environment full of distractions.
- Executive function and attention control.

So you should not explain every case of procrastination with a single line:

**"My brain is short of dopamine."**

Human behaviour is far more complex.

## Conclusion

🧠 **A Dopamine Detox is not about flushing dopamine out of the brain.**

Dopamine is not a toxin, nor is it the source of laziness. It is an important part of the system that helps us learn, seek rewards and stay motivated.

What is worth controlling is not dopamine but **the habits that constantly exploit the brain's reward system**.

Instead of trying to "reset your brain" in 24 hours, a more sustainable approach is to redesign your environment:

**less unintended stimulation, fewer automatic behaviours and more periods of deliberate focus.**

The goal is not to live a life without dopamine.

The goal is for **you to choose the rewards, instead of letting the rewards choose your behaviour**.

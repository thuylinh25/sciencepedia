## What is light, really?

Light is **[[buc-xa-dien-tu|electromagnetic radiation]]**, in the same family as radio waves, microwaves, infrared, ultraviolet, X-rays and gamma rays.

Light shows wave properties, and it can also be described as quanta of energy called **[[photon|photons]]**.

The human eye perceives only a very small part of the electromagnetic spectrum, called **[[anh-sang-kha-kien|visible light]]**, with wavelengths of about:

- Violet: near 380 nm
- Blue: about 450 nm
- Green: about 530 nm
- Red: near 700 nm

White light, such as sunlight, is a combination of many different wavelengths.

## From light to the retina

When you look at a red rose, the petals absorb some wavelengths and more strongly reflect the wavelengths that our visual system perceives as red.

The reflected light passes through the cornea and the lens before being focused onto the **[[vong-mac|retina]]**, the light-sensitive layer of tissue at the back of the eyeball.

The retina does not simply "take a photo". It starts processing information as soon as light enters the eye.

## The cells that turn light into nerve signals

The retina contains two important groups of light-sensing cells.

### Rod cells

**[[te-bao-cam-thu-anh-sang|Rod cells]] (rods)** are very sensitive to dim light and help us:

- See in the dark.
- Distinguish brightness.
- Detect motion in low light.

However, they provide almost no information about colour.

### Cone cells

**Cone cells (cones)** work well when there is enough light and are mainly responsible for colour vision.

Humans usually have three types of cone cell:

- **S**: sensitive to short wavelengths.
- **M**: sensitive to medium wavelengths.
- **L**: sensitive to long wavelengths.

The brain compares how strongly these three cell types are stimulated to create the perception of countless colours.

## From photons to electrochemical signals

When a photon is absorbed by a photoreceptor cell, a chain of biochemical reactions takes place and turns the light information into **electrochemical nerve signals**.

These signals are processed further through several layers of cells in the retina before passing into:

**The optic nerve**

The optic nerve carries information from the eye to the brain.

At this point, there is no "tiny picture" travelling along the nerve. There are only nerve signals that encode information about light, contrast, colour and many other features.

## The brain creates the world we see

Visual information is sent to many areas of the brain, among which the **visual cortex in the occipital lobe** plays an especially important role.

The brain processes, all at once:

- Colour.
- Shape.
- Outlines.
- Motion.
- Contrast.
- Distance and depth.
- The position of objects.

The information is then combined with memory and experience to recognise what is in front of us.

A collection of photons finally becomes the experience:

**"That is a red rose."**

## Does colour really exist out there?

Outside the brain there is light with different wavelengths and energies.

But **"red", "blue" and "yellow" are sensory experiences created by the nervous system as it processes light**.

An object does not contain "redness" the way it contains iron or carbon atoms. It reflects and absorbs light in particular ways, and the brain turns that information into the experience of colour.

This is also why animals with visual systems different from ours may experience the same scene very differently.

## Do the eyes see, or does the brain?

The most accurate way to put it is:

**The eye collects light → the retina converts and processes the signals → the optic nerve carries the information → the brain creates the visual experience.**

👁️ The colourful world before your eyes is not a photo copied intact into your head. It is the result of millions of nerve cells working together to turn photons into colour, shape, depth and, finally, a meaningful world.

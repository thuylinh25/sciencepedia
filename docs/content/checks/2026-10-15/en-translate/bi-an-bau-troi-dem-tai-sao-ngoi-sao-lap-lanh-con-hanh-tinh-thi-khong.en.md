## The culprit: Earth's turbulent atmosphere

To understand this phenomenon, we first have to look at Earth's atmosphere, not at the stars themselves.

The atmosphere is not a still layer of air. Winds and thermal currents constantly stir together layers of air at different temperatures, and therefore at different densities. The refractive index of air depends on its density, so where air masses mix, countless small pockets with different refractive indices form, changing continuously in both space and time.

Light from a very distant object reaches the top of the atmosphere almost as a flat wavefront. As it passes through those pockets of differing refractive index, different parts of the wavefront are slowed and deflected unevenly. The change in direction of light as it passes through a medium with a different refractive index is called **[[khuc-xa-anh-sang|refraction of light]]**.

## Why do stars twinkle?

### A point of light with almost no size

Stars are giant balls of plasma that shine by themselves thanks to [[phan-ung-nhiet-hach|nuclear fusion]] in their cores (see also [Stars: structure and life cycle](/articles/ngoi-sao-cau-tao-va-vong-doi)). But they are very far away: the star nearest the Sun, [Proxima Centauri](/articles/neu-roi-he-mat-troi-proxima-centauri-se-la-diem-dung-dau-tien), is already more than 4 [[nam-anh-sang|light-years]] from us, and every other star is farther still. At such distances, to our eyes, each star is just a **point of light**.

### The twinkling effect

The atmosphere acts on starlight in two ways. The first tilts parts of the wavefront, so the star's image is blurred and shifts slightly. The second causes twinkling: pockets of air act like very weak lenses, focusing the light in some places and spreading it out in others. By the time it reaches the ground, starlight is no longer evenly distributed but broken into small patches of light and shadow, carried along by winds high up. When a bright patch sweeps across the eye, the star brightens; when a dark patch sweeps across, the star dims.

This rapid, continuous fluctuation in brightness is called **stellar twinkling** (*stellar scintillation*). It is caused mainly by turbulence high in the atmosphere, not by the layer of air close to the ground.

## Why don't planets twinkle?

### A planet is a disc, even if the naked eye cannot tell

Planets do not shine by themselves; they reflect sunlight. They lie within the Solar System, much closer to us than the stars, so they span a far larger angle on the sky: each planet is a **small bright disc**, not a point.

To the naked eye, that disc is still too small to make out, so a planet still looks like a dot of light. A pair of binoculars is enough to see that a planet is not as small as the stars.

### The self-cancelling effect

Light from each part of a planet's disc is also made to fluctuate by the atmosphere, but different parts do not fluctuate in step. When one part dims, another brightens, and the fluctuations average each other out. The total amount of light reaching the eye stays almost constant, so the planet looks steady, twinkling little or not at all.

Interestingly, this effect "sees" more finely than the eye. Astronomers have pointed out that the mere fact that stars twinkle while planets do not reveals the difference in angular size between them, at a level of detail about ten times finer than the resolution of the human eye.

## Quick comparison of stars and planets

| Feature | Star | Planet |
|-----------|------------|------------|
| **Light source** | Shines by itself through nuclear fusion. | Reflects sunlight. |
| **Distance from Earth** | Very far; even the nearest is more than 4 light-years away. | Relatively close, within the Solar System. |
| **Size on the sky** | Almost a point. | A small disc; too small for the naked eye to make out, but visible through binoculars. |
| **Effect of the atmosphere** | Brightness fluctuates rapidly, so it twinkles. | Fluctuations from different parts of the disc cancel out, so it shines steadily. |
| **To the naked eye** | Usually twinkles clearly, especially when low in the sky. | Usually shines steadily; twinkles only slightly when very low. |

## A few tips for telling stars from planets

Without a telescope, you can still make a fairly confident guess with the naked eye.

### 1. Watch how steady the light is

- A point of light that flickers constantly: very likely a **star**.
- A bright, steady point that barely flickers while high in the sky: probably a **planet**.

### 2. Check its position relative to the ecliptic

Over a year, the Sun travels across the sky along an apparent path called the **[[hoang-dao|ecliptic]]**, which lies in the plane of Earth's orbit. The planets always appear near this line, because every planet in the Solar System formed from the same flat disc of gas and dust rotating around the Sun. The constellations lying along that path are [the zodiac constellations](/articles/cac-chom-sao-hoang-dao).

If you see a very bright object that does not twinkle and lies near the ecliptic, there is a good chance it is a planet such as [Venus](/articles/sao-kim-bai-hoc-ve-hieu-ung-nha-kinh-mat-kiem-soat) or [Jupiter](/articles/sao-moc-nguoi-khong-lo-khi-va-tam-khien-cua-he).

## An exception: near the horizon

When an object is low near the horizon, its light must pass through a much thicker layer of atmosphere than when it is overhead, and twinkling grows stronger very quickly as the object sinks lower. At such times, even a planet may twinkle slightly. This is why Venus or Jupiter sometimes still seems to "twinkle" just after rising or shortly before setting.

A star that is low in the sky can also flash in changing colours. The atmosphere bends light of different colours by slightly different amounts, so each colour twinkles to its own rhythm, not in step with the others, and this offset grows as the star sinks lower.

## Why do space telescopes matter?

Twinkling makes the night sky more beautiful, but the atmosphere causes trouble for astronomers in several ways. It blurs images, limiting the sharpness of ground-based telescopes. It makes the brightness of stars fluctuate, adding noise when brightness has to be measured very precisely, for example when searching for planets outside the Solar System as they pass in front of their stars. These two effects are related but distinct: blurring comes mainly from the strongest layer of turbulence, usually near the ground, while twinkling comes mainly from turbulence high up, so there are nights when images are blurred yet brightness stays fairly stable.

Orbiting Earth at an altitude of about 480 km, the **Hubble** telescope avoids the distortions caused by the atmosphere. For [the **James Webb** telescope](/articles/kinh-james-webb-nhin-nguoc-ve-thuo-vu-tru-so-sinh), the main reason is different: at most of the infrared wavelengths Webb observes, the atmosphere blocks almost all the light and glows strongly itself, so a cold telescope placed in space is needed.

Seen from outside the atmosphere, stars no longer twinkle the way they do when we observe them from the ground.

## Conclusion

A star twinkles not because it flickers itself, but because its light must pass through an atmosphere that is always turbulent. A planet is subject to the same atmosphere, but it is a small disc rather than a point, so the fluctuations from different parts of the disc cancel each other out and the planet looks steady.

So on a clear night, if you see a brilliant point of light high in the sky that hardly twinkles at all, it is very likely a planet in the Solar System, not a distant star.

*The night sky is not only beautiful but also a physics laboratory. Just by looking up, you can directly observe how light interacts with Earth's atmosphere.*

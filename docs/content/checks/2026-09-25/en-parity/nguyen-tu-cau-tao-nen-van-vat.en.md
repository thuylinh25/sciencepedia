## The nucleus: where almost all the mass sits

The nucleus is made of:

- **[[proton|Protons]]**, which carry a positive charge.
- **Neutrons**, which carry no charge.

A proton or a neutron has about 1,840 times the mass of an electron, so almost all of an atom's mass is in its nucleus.

Although it holds nearly all the mass, the nucleus is extremely small compared with the overall size of the atom.

## Electrons set the "size" of the atom

Electrons do not travel along fixed orbits, as simple illustrations suggest.

Instead, they occupy regions of space described by quantum mechanics, called **orbitals**.

Electrons are arranged in energy shells, and each shell divides into subshells labelled:

- s
- p
- d
- f

This arrangement is what determines the chemical properties of each element.

## Two numbers that identify every atom

### Atomic number (Z)

Z is the number of protons in the nucleus.

This is the number that decides which chemical element the atom is.

Examples:

- Hydrogen: Z = 1
- Carbon: Z = 6
- Oxygen: Z = 8

Change the number of protons and the atom becomes a completely different element.

### Mass number (A)

The mass number equals:

> protons + neutrons

The number of neutrons is:

> A − Z

Two atoms with the same Z but a different number of neutrons are called **[[dong-vi|isotopes]]**.

## Isotopes: same element, different neutron count

Carbon always has 6 protons, but it exists as several isotopes:

- **Carbon-12 (¹²C)**: 6 neutrons
- **Carbon-13 (¹³C)**: 7 neutrons
- **Carbon-14 (¹⁴C)**: 8 neutrons

Of these:

- Carbon-12 and carbon-13 are stable isotopes.
- Carbon-14 is radioactive, and is used to date ancient samples.

## Why don't atomic masses add up exactly?

If you add up the separate masses of all the protons, neutrons and electrons in a carbon-12 atom, the result is larger than the atom's actual mass.

This "missing" mass is called the:

> **mass defect**

Through Einstein's famous equation:

> E = mc²

the missing mass corresponds to the **[[nang-luong-lien-ket-hat-nhan|nuclear binding energy]]** — the energy given off when the protons and neutrons came together, and the energy that would have to be supplied to pull them apart again.

## Where does nuclear energy come from?

Every nucleus has a definite binding energy.

When light nuclei combine in **[[phan-ung-nhiet-hach|fusion]]**, or a heavy nucleus splits in **fission**, the resulting nuclei are more tightly bound than the ones that went in, and the difference is released as energy.

That energy:

- Supplies the Sun's light and heat.
- Runs nuclear power plants.
- Drives nuclear weapons, the most powerful explosions humans have ever produced.

Protons, neutrons and electrons are invisible to the naked eye, yet they make up all the ordinary matter in the universe. From the simplest atoms to giant stars, all of it is built from these particles.

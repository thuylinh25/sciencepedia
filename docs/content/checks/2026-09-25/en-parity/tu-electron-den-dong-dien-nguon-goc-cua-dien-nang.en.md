## Electric charge: the basic "counting unit" of electricity

All matter is made of particles that carry electric charge.

- **Electrons** carry a negative charge.
- **[[proton|Protons]]** carry a positive charge.
- The charges of the proton and the electron are equal in magnitude and opposite in sign.

The smallest charge that exists freely in nature is called the **elementary charge** (*e*):

> e = 1.602176634 × 10⁻¹⁹ coulomb

The unit of electric charge is the **coulomb (C)**.

One coulomb corresponds to about:

> 6.24 × 10¹⁸ elementary charges

In other words, the charge on a single electron is tiny: the currents we use every day are made of enormous numbers of moving electrons.

## What is current measured in?

The unit of electric current is the **ampere (A)**.

One ampere is a current that carries:

> 1 coulomb of charge per second

Or:

> 1 A = 1 C/s

This lets scientists measure and compare currents precisely all over the world.

## How has the ampere been defined?

### The old definition

For many years, the ampere was defined through the magnetic force between two parallel wires.

If two wires placed 1 metre apart in a vacuum felt a very small, specified force between them, the current in the wires was used to define the ampere.

This definition was hard to realise in practice, however, because it is impossible to make the "infinitely long" wires the theoretical model calls for.

### The modern definition

Since 2019, the SI has defined the ampere directly in terms of the **elementary charge of the electron**.

This makes the electrical units more stable and no longer dependent on mechanical measurements as before.

## Is the direction of current really the direction electrons move?

One curious fact:

- Conventional current flows from the positive terminal to the negative terminal.
- The electrons in a wire actually move from the negative terminal to the positive terminal.

The reason is that the convention for the direction of current was set before scientists discovered the electron.

Even though we know electrons move the opposite way, the old convention has been kept so that science and engineering stay consistent.

## Current does not only flow in wires

### Lightning

A lightning flash is a huge electric current that shoots through the air when the insulating capacity of the atmosphere breaks down.

A typical lightning flash can reach:

- A voltage of about **300 million volts**
- A current of about **30,000 amperes**

Far more than ordinary household electricity.

### Aurorae

The Sun continually sends out streams of charged particles, made up of electrons and protons.

When these particles are channelled by Earth's magnetic field into the upper atmosphere near the poles and collide with the gases there, they produce colourful **aurorae**.

### The human nervous system

Even our bodies run on tiny electric currents.

Nerve cells send signals by controlling the flow of ions such as:

- Sodium (Na⁺)
- Potassium (K⁺)
- Calcium (Ca²⁺)

These ionic currents let the brain communicate with the muscles and the body's organs.

## Charge and current: the core relationship

Charge and current are like water and a river:

- **Charge** is the "particles" that carry electricity.
- **Current** is the flow of those charges.

When charges are at rest, they produce only an electric field. When they start to move, they form a current and also produce a magnetic field.

This simple relationship underlies all modern electrical technology, from light bulbs and electric motors to computers and the Internet.

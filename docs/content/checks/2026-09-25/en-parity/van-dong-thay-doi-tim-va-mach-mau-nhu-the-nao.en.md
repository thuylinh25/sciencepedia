The heart is a muscle, and like any other muscle, it adapts to repeated loads.

## How the heart changes

In people who do regular endurance training, the left ventricle becomes larger and its muscular wall thickens moderately. As a result, **stroke volume** — the amount of blood pumped with each beat — rises considerably.

Because the body needs a certain blood flow at rest, a heart that pumps more per beat needs fewer beats. This is why the resting heart rate of endurance athletes is often 40–50 beats per minute, compared with 60–80 in inactive people.

This physiological adaptation must be distinguished from **pathological hypertrophy** caused by [[tang-huyet-ap|hypertension]]: the shape of the heart, its diastolic function and its capacity to recover are quite different.

## Blood vessels

The layer of cells lining the inside of blood vessels — the **endothelium** — releases nitric oxide, which widens the vessels. Faster blood flow during exercise creates shear stress on the vessel wall, and that stress is what stimulates this process.

Regular training improves endothelial function, and this is considered one of the mechanisms that explain why physical activity lowers blood pressure. A meta-analysis of 93 trials by Cornelissen and Smart (2013) found that in people who **already had hypertension**, endurance training lowered systolic blood pressure by an average of **8.3 mmHg** (confidence limits 6.0–10.7) and diastolic blood pressure by 5.2 mmHg. In people with normal blood pressure the change was far smaller — about 0.75 mmHg systolic, with a confidence interval that includes zero.

## VO₂max: a strong predictor

VO₂max is the maximum amount of oxygen the body can use per minute. In large cohort studies it is one of the strongest predictors of death from all causes — stronger than smoking, hypertension or diabetes in some analyses.

The good news: the largest improvement in risk comes from moving from "completely inactive" to "a little active". Going from 0 to 90 minutes a week already lowers risk considerably; going from 300 to 400 minutes adds a much smaller benefit.

## Current recommendations

The WHO recommends that adults do:

- 150–300 minutes of moderate-intensity activity per week, **or** 75–150 minutes of vigorous-intensity activity
- Plus muscle-strengthening activity for the major muscle groups on 2 or more days a week
- Less continuous sitting — break it up with a few minutes of standing and walking

## Exercise does not replace the medication you take

Because regular training can lower blood pressure, some people start thinking about stopping their medication. **Do not stop on your own.** Treatment guidelines place exercise as a measure *alongside* medication, not as a replacement for it; stopping blood-pressure medication abruptly can make blood pressure rebound higher than before and raise the risk of stroke. If training has brought your blood pressure down, that is a reason for your doctor to consider adjusting the dose — and adjusting the dose is your doctor's decision.

See a doctor before you start if you have known cardiovascular disease, uncontrolled hypertension or diabetes, or if you have not been active for a long time.

**Stop exercising and seek medical care immediately if during exertion you notice:** chest pain or tightness, shortness of breath far greater than the effort you are making, dizziness or fainting, an irregular heartbeat, or pain spreading to the jaw, neck or arm.

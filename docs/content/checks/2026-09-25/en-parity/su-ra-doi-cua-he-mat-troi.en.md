## Three features to explain

Any model of how the Solar System originated has to explain:

1. Why the planets lie almost in the same plane and all orbit the Sun in the same direction (anticlockwise, seen from above the Sun's north pole).
2. Why the rocky planets are in the inner region and the giant planets in the outer region.
3. Why the Sun holds **more than 99% of the system's mass** but only a very small fraction of its **[[dong-luong-goc|angular momentum]]** (a measure of how much an object is "spinning" around a point or an axis).

The angular momentum problem is the hardest of the three.

## How did the Solar System begin?

A giant molecular cloud became unstable and began to collapse under its own gravity, possibly triggered by the shock wave from a nearby [[sieu-tan-tinh|supernova]].

As it contracted:

- The cloud spun faster and faster, because angular momentum is conserved.
- It gradually flattened into a **protoplanetary disc** (a huge disc of gas and dust orbiting a young star).
- Most of the material gathered at the centre and formed the young Sun.

Discs like this can now be observed around other young stars. ALMA, for example, has imaged the disc around the young star HL Tau in fine detail, revealing a pattern of bright and dark rings that may be linked to planet formation.

## How did the planets form?

The planets did not separate out of "rings of material", as classical models once proposed.

Instead:

1. Dust grains collided and stuck together.
2. Objects metres to kilometres across formed.
3. These objects kept colliding and merging.
4. Repeated over time, this built up the planets.

This process is called **[[boi-tu|accretion]]**.

## Why are there rocky planets and gas giants?

The key is the **snow line** — more precisely the water snow line; ammonia and methane freeze out only much farther from the Sun.

### Inside the snow line

It is too hot for water ice to condense, and hotter still for ammonia and methane ice.

Only rock and metal exist in solid form.

With little solid material available, the planets that formed were small and became:

- Mercury.
- Venus.
- Earth.
- Mars.

### Outside the snow line

Ice can condense as well.

In the leading model, known as **core accretion**, the much larger supply of solid material lets planetary cores grow quickly and become massive enough to pull in huge amounts of gas from the protoplanetary disc.

The result:

- Jupiter.
- Saturn.
- Uranus.
- Neptune.

## The angular momentum problem

The puzzle is this:

- The Sun holds almost all of the mass.
- Yet most of the angular momentum is in the planets.

Researchers have proposed mechanisms that would have carried angular momentum away from the young Sun, slowing its spin. Two of the main ones:

### Magnetic braking

The young Sun's magnetic field interacted with the surrounding disc of gas and transferred angular momentum outwards.

### The solar wind

Charged particles, dragged along by the magnetic field as they leave, carry angular momentum away over billions of years.

## Planetary migration

A separate finding is that the planets did not form exactly where they are today. Migration moves angular momentum between the planets and the disc; it does not by itself explain why the Sun spins slowly.

Early on:

- Jupiter and Saturn may have shifted their orbits significantly.

## Exoplanets have changed our view

The discovery of more than 6,000 exoplanets (NASA count, as of September 2026) shows that the Solar System is not the only template.

Astronomers have found:

- Hot Jupiters orbiting extremely close to their parent stars.
- "Super-Earths", larger than Earth but smaller than Neptune.
- Many systems with orbits very different from those of the Solar System.

This suggests that the same physical processes can produce very different outcomes, depending strongly on each system's initial conditions and history.

## Conclusion

The Solar System formed from a cloud of gas and dust about **4.57 billion years ago**. Gravity built the Sun, dust grains clumped together into planets, and the temperature distribution in the protoplanetary disc largely decided which planets would be rocky and which would be giants. Today, the protoplanetary discs observed around young stars suggest that the process that built the Solar System is still going on across the Milky Way.

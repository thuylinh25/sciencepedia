## What is a galaxy?

A galaxy typically contains:

- Anywhere from a few thousand to trillions of stars (NASA: the smallest galaxies hold a few thousand stars, the largest hold trillions).
- Gas and dust between the stars.
- A large amount of surrounding [[vat-chat-toi|dark matter]].
- Often a supermassive black hole at the center.

Galaxy sizes vary enormously:

- **The smallest galaxies**: only a few hundred [[nam-anh-sang|light-years]] across.
- **The Milky Way**: a stellar disk spanning more than 100,000 light-years.
- **The largest galaxies**: more than a million light-years across.

## Galaxy classification

### Spiral galaxies

The most familiar type, consisting of:

- A disk of stars rotating around the center.
- Spiral arms rich in gas and dust.
- Sites of vigorous star formation.

The Milky Way belongs to the **barred spiral** group.

**Andromeda (M31)** is the nearest large galaxy to us, about 2.5 million light-years away (NASA/Hubble).

### Elliptical galaxies

- Spherical or oval in shape.
- Made mostly of old stars.
- Contain little gas and dust.
- Form almost no new stars.

Many elliptical galaxies are thought to be the result of mergers between large galaxies.

### Lenticular galaxies

An intermediate form between spiral and elliptical galaxies.

- A distinct stellar disk.
- Almost no spiral arms.
- Little gas and dust, so a low rate of star formation.

### Irregular galaxies

They lack a clear symmetrical structure.

The usual causes:

- Galaxy collisions.
- Gravitational influence from neighboring galaxies.

Two well-known examples are the **Large Magellanic Cloud** and the **Small Magellanic Cloud**, satellite galaxies of the Milky Way.

## What do galaxy shapes tell us?

A galaxy's current shape reflects its history:

- How many collisions it has undergone.
- How many smaller galaxies it has swallowed.
- How much gas remains to keep forming stars.

Galaxies are therefore not unchanging structures; they evolve continuously over time.

## The future of the Milky Way

The Milky Way and Andromeda are moving toward each other.

What will happen is still debated, and two recent research groups read the same kind of data in opposite directions:

- Sawala and colleagues (2025) simulated the whole Local Group, including the gravitational influence of the Large Magellanic Cloud and M33, and concluded that the probability of **no** merger within the next 10 billion years is close to 50% — two roughly equal possibilities, not a foregone outcome.
- Wu and colleagues (2026) used the latest Gaia proper-motion data (corrected for systematic errors) within the same modeling framework and obtained a merger probability of **90%**, with a median merger time of about **6.5 billion years** (+1.3/−1.5).

Wu and colleagues state explicitly that their result is not yet conclusive: across the 2σ uncertainty region the probability ranges from 64.7% to 100%, and more precise proper-motion measurements (around 2 microarcseconds per year) will be needed to settle it.

If the two spiral galaxies do merge, the product will most likely be an elliptical galaxy: simulations by Cox and Loeb show that the density profiles of stars, gas and dark matter in the merger product "resemble those of elliptical galaxies", and they treat the Local Group as "a prototype progenitor of late-forming elliptical galaxies".

🌌 Galaxies are not merely giant collections of stars. Their shapes are archives of billions of years of evolution, collisions and mergers in the universe.

# What Is Energy?

Energy is everywhere in nature, from sunlight and the current in a wire to the movement of the human body. In physics, energy is often described as a **quantity that measures the capacity to bring about change or to do work** — a useful working description rather than a rigorous definition.

The most important point is this:

> In an isolated system, energy is neither created nor destroyed; it only changes from one form to another.

## What is energy measured in?

The SI unit of energy is the **[[joule]] (J)**.

One joule is the work done when a force of 1 newton pushes an object through 1 metre.

Besides the joule, we also meet:

- the **electronvolt (eV)** in particle physics;
- the **kilowatt-hour (kWh)** on an electricity bill.

## Common forms of energy

### Kinetic energy

The energy of a moving object.

Examples:

- A car on the road.
- A ball in flight.
- The rotating Earth.

### Potential energy

Energy stored because of an object's position.

Examples:

- An object held up high.
- Water held behind a hydroelectric dam.

### Thermal energy

Energy associated with the motion of atoms and molecules.

The hotter an object, the more vigorously the particles inside it move.

### Chemical energy

Energy stored in chemical bonds.

Examples:

- Food.
- Batteries.
- Petrol and other fuels.

### Electrical energy

Energy associated with the movement of electric charges.

Electrical energy runs most modern devices.

### Nuclear energy

Energy stored in the nucleus of an atom.

It is the energy source of the Sun and of nuclear power plants.

## How does energy change form?

In nature, energy is constantly changing form.

Examples:

- Sunlight → chemical energy in green plants.
- Chemical energy in food → kinetic energy of muscles.
- Kinetic energy → thermal energy through friction.

The form of energy can change, but the total amount of energy is conserved.

## How are mass and energy related?

According to Einstein's special theory of relativity:

> E = mc²

The equation expresses mass–energy equivalence: a change in a body's energy comes with a corresponding change in its mass, and the reverse.

Because c² is enormous, a very small amount of mass corresponds to a very large amount of energy — which is why nuclear reactions release so much energy.

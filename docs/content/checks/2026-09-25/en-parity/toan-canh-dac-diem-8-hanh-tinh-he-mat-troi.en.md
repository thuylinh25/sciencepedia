A planet is a sub-stellar body. Its mass is not enough to ignite [[phan-ung-nhiet-hach|nuclear fusion]] in its core, so a planet does not shine by itself — what we see is only light from its parent star reflected back. Most planets orbit a star or a stellar system, although some planets drift freely between the stars.

The Solar System has eight planets. The number sounds obvious, but it was only settled in 2006.

## The three conditions for a planet

The problem is that a great many bodies in the Solar System orbit the Sun. In August 2006 the International Astronomical Union (IAU) adopted an official definition made up of three conditions that must all be met at once:

1. **It is in orbit around the Sun.**
2. **It has enough mass for its self-gravity to overcome the strength of its material**, pulling it into hydrostatic equilibrium — in other words, it must be nearly round.
3. **It has cleared the neighbourhood around its orbit** (IAU resolution 2006, condition c). Put briefly: no other body of comparable size shares its orbit, apart from its own moons and bodies locked in resonance.

The third condition is the controversial one, and it is the one that removed Pluto from the list. Pluto is round and orbits the Sun, but it shares its orbital zone with countless other bodies in the [[vanh-dai-kuiper|Kuiper belt]].

Pluto was placed in a new category: **[[hanh-tinh-lun|dwarf planets]]**. This category currently has five recognized members (as of September 2026) — Pluto, Ceres (the largest body in the asteroid belt), Eris, Haumea and Makemake — plus a number of candidates awaiting confirmation.

## Two very different groups

The eight planets fall into two distinct groups:

**The inner group** — Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars — are rocky planets with solid surfaces, small sizes and masses, and few or no moons.

**The outer group** — Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, Neptune — are giant planets with no solid surface to stand on, and each brings along a whole system of moons. Uranus and Neptune are split off into a smaller branch called **ice giants**, because beneath their gas layers lies a mixture of water, methane and ammonia at enormous pressure, rather than the metallic hydrogen of the other two.

The boundary between the two groups is the asteroid belt, and that is no coincidence: in the protoplanetary disk, only far enough from the Sun could water and other volatiles freeze, allowing planetary cores to grow fast enough and heavy enough to hold on to gas.

One AU (astronomical unit) is the mean distance from Earth to the Sun, about 149.6 million km. The distance column uses the mean (J2000) orbital elements from the NASA Planetary Fact Sheet — that is the mean semi-major axis, not the instantaneous distance: planetary orbits are ellipses, so the actual distance varies around this value. The moon counts for the four outer planets change with each round of new discoveries. The figures in the table are confirmed moons according to NASA as of August 2026 — not named moons, which are far fewer. Saturn's count jumped after the IAU Minor Planet Center recognized 128 additional moons in a single announcement in March 2025.

Two things stand out in the table. Venus takes 243 days to rotate once, longer than the 225 days it takes to complete an orbit — but that is the sidereal rotation period, not the length of a day. Because Venus rotates backwards, a solar day there is only about 117 days, which is shorter than its year. And both Venus and Uranus rotate in the opposite direction to the rest.

## Where the names come from

The international names of the planets come from Roman mythology. Many of the names match an observable feature — but that reflects how the namers chose their gods, not a rule: some names relate to colour (Mars, Neptune), some to speed (Mercury), and some simply to a high-ranking god (Jupiter).

- **Mercury** is the messenger god, swift as the wind — Mercury has the shortest orbital period and quickly returns to the same position relative to the Sun.
- **Venus** is the goddess of beauty — Venus is the brightest object in the night sky after the Moon. The same planet is called *Sao Mai* in Vietnamese when it rises before dawn on the eastern horizon, and *Sao Hôm* when it sets after dusk on the western horizon.
- **Mars** is the god of war — the red colour of iron oxide on its surface.
- **Jupiter** is the supreme god — the largest planet in the system.
- **Saturn** is the father of Jupiter, and **Uranus** is the father of Saturn: the mythological lineage runs backwards as you move outward from the Sun. This is a coincidence of naming, not of discovery history — Jupiter and Saturn have been known since antiquity, while Uranus was the FIRST planet found with a telescope (William Herschel, 1781).
- **Neptune** is the god of the sea; the planet is also sea-blue, although that colour comes from methane in its atmosphere, not from any connection to the sea.

In East Asian tradition, the five planets visible to the naked eye are named after the five elements — Water, Metal, Fire, Wood, Earth — based on the colours and qualities the ancients assigned to them.

## A ninth planet?

In 2016, Konstantin Batygin and Michael E. Brown (Caltech) published indirect evidence for an unknown planet very far away: the orbits of a group of Kuiper belt objects cluster in a way that is hard to explain without a large mass acting on them. To date that body has never been observed directly, so it is not on the official list — and even the inference is disputed: some studies argue that the structure of the Kuiper belt itself, rather than a hidden planet, is enough to explain the clustering.

> Note on spelling: in Vietnamese, planet names capitalize both words — *Sao Thuỷ*, *Sao Kim* — to distinguish them from "sao" (star) in the sense of modern astronomy. A planet is not a star.

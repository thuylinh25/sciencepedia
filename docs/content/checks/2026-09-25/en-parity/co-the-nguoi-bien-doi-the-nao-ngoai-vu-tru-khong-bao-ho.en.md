## First 0–15 Seconds: Oxygen Deprivation and Loss of Consciousness

There is no air in space.

- Oxygen in the blood is rapidly used up.
- The brain quickly becomes severely starved of oxygen.
- After about **10–15 seconds**, a person loses consciousness. In a vacuum-chamber accident at NASA's Manned Spacecraft Center (Houston) in the mid-1960s, the test subject remained conscious for about 14 seconds (roughly the time it takes oxygen-depleted blood to travel from the lungs to the brain) and came round after the chamber was repressurized.

If you try to hold your breath, the air in your lungs expands as the outside pressure drops suddenly. This can tear lung tissue and cause severe lung injury or death.

## The Next Few Tens of Seconds: The Body Begins to Swell

Due to the near-vacuum environment:

- External pressure drops to near zero.
- Fluids in the tissues begin to form gas bubbles.

This phenomenon is known as **ebullism** (boiling caused by a drop in pressure).

The body may:

- Swell noticeably.
- Increase significantly in volume.

However, the body **does not explode** as many movies depict, because the skin is elastic enough to hold the internal tissues in.

## Fluids Begin to Boil

When pressure drops sharply:

- Saliva bubbles.
- Tears evaporate rapidly.
- Fluids in the tissues begin to turn into water vapor.

Blood in the large vessels boils more slowly than other body fluids because the circulatory system keeps it under pressure, but it is not protected indefinitely: water vapor forms fastest in the soft tissues and somewhat more slowly in the venous blood. The bubbles that form cause severe damage to the body.

## No Instant Freezing

A common misconception is that a person would instantly freeze solid in space.

In reality:

- A vacuum conducts heat very poorly.
- There is no air to draw heat away from the body quickly.

Therefore, the body does not freeze right away.

As for direct sunlight:

- The two figures often quoted, above **120°C** in sunlight and below **−150°C** in shade, describe the conditions that spacewalks take place in, not the temperature a human body would reach.
- The body loses heat mainly through water evaporating from the airways and the skin, so the skin would feel slightly cool rather than heating up or freezing over the time span discussed here.

## Cosmic Radiation

On Earth, we are protected by:

- The atmosphere.
- The ozone layer.
- Earth's magnetic field.

In outer space:

- Ultraviolet radiation strikes the skin directly.
- X-rays, high-energy [[proton|protons]] and cosmic rays can pass through the body.

With prolonged exposure, cells suffer severe radiation damage.

## After 1–2 Minutes: Death

If the person is not returned to a pressurized, oxygenated environment:

- Brain damage becomes irreversible.
- The circulatory system collapses.
- Oxygen deprivation becomes total.

Death usually occurs within **1–2 minutes**.

## Can a Person Be Rescued?

Yes.

If the victim is returned to an environment with adequate pressure and oxygen in **less than about 1 minute**, recovery is still possible and may leave no severe lasting damage.


🌌 Space does not kill with instant cold, as movies often show. The greatest hazards are **vacuum and oxygen deprivation**, which cause unconsciousness within seconds and death within one to two minutes unless the person is rescued in time.
